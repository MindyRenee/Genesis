pub fn new(dimension: usize, c: f64) -> Result<Self, FieldError>

The κ-stereographic sphere: the sphere of constant positive curvature κ, charted by stereographic projection. The origin of the chart is the image of the north pole — the distinguished center of the model. The point at infinity is the south pole, the one point the chart cannot represent.

contains/project enforce the field’s operating region — the hemisphere cap around the pole, ‖x‖ < 1/√κ (the stereographic image of the equator). The cap is geodesically convex: minimizing geodesics between cap points stay in the cap, and any set of cap points has a unique Fréchet mean inside it (Buss & Fillmore 2001). The geometric primitives (mobius_add, log_*, exp_*, geodesic) accept any valid chart point — finite and right dimension — and return honest sphere points, which may legitimately lie outside the cap; the state boundaries re-project.

pub fn distance(&self, x: &[f64], y: &[f64]) -> Result<f64, FieldError>

Geodesic distance on the sphere. In stereographic coordinates, with û = √κ·x the unit-sphere chart, the lifted chord gives

tan(d·√κ/2) = √κ‖x − y‖ / √(1 + 2κ⟨x,y⟩ + κ²‖x‖²‖y‖²)

so d = (2/√κ)·atan2(√κ‖x−y‖, √(1+2κ⟨x,y⟩+κ²‖x‖²‖y‖²)). The denominator factors as (1+κ⟨x,y⟩)² + κ²(‖x‖²‖y‖² − ⟨x,y⟩²) — nonnegative by Cauchy–Schwarz, vanishing only for antipodal pairs, where atan2 correctly returns the maximal distance π/√κ instead of dividing by zero.

Source

pub fn log_origin(&self, x: &[f64]) -> Result<Vector, FieldError>

The tangent at the pole (the chart’s origin) pointing along the geodesic to x, distance-normalized: ‖v‖ = d(0, x). A point at chart radius r sits at geodesic distance (2/√κ)·atan(√κ·r) from the pole — unlike the ball’s atanh this needs no clamp: atan is finite for every representable point, tending to π/(2√κ) as r → ∞ (the south pole).

Source

pub fn exp_origin(&self, v: &[f64]) -> Result<Vector, FieldError>

Step from the pole along direction v for ‖v‖ units of geodesic distance: the image is the direction of v at chart radius (1/√κ)·tan(√κ·‖v‖/2). tan is honest about the sphere being compact: ‖v‖ = π/√κ lands at the unrepresentable south pole (rejected as non-finite), and beyond it the map wraps around — the sphere closes on itself.

Source

pub fn mobius_add(&self, x: &[f64], y: &[f64]) -> Result<Vector, FieldError>

Möbius addition — the gyrovector operation that plays the role of vector addition on the stereographic sphere (Ungar; κ > 0, equator image at radius 1/√κ):

x ⊕_κ y = [(1 − 2κ⟨x,y⟩ − κ‖y‖²)x + (1 + κ‖x‖²)y] / (1 − 2κ⟨x,y⟩ + κ²‖x‖²‖y‖²)

Non-commutative and non-associative, but left-cancellative: (−x) ⊕_κ (x ⊕_κ y) = y — the identity that makes the general log/exp pair below true inverses. Adding a fixed element is a spherical translation — a rotation of the sphere, so it moves every point the same geodesic distance. The denominator can only vanish when y is the antipode of −x (the sum would be the unrepresentable south pole); inside the cap it is bounded away from zero by (1 − κ‖x‖‖y‖)².

Source

fn mobius_sub(&self, x: &[f64], y: &[f64]) -> Result<Vector, FieldError>

Möbius subtraction: (−x) ⊕_κ y — the displacement from x to y expressed as a chart point. A spherical translation of y back toward the origin: d(0, −x ⊕ y) = d(x, y).

Source

pub fn log_at(&self, x: &[f64], y: &[f64]) -> Result<Vector, FieldError>

Logarithm map at an arbitrary basepoint: the tangent vector at x that points along the geodesic toward y, with the same distance-normalized convention as log_origin (‖v‖ = the geodesic distance the vector travels). Because x ↦ (−x) ⊕ x is a spherical translation to the origin, this is just log_origin of the Möbius displacement.

Source

pub fn exp_at(&self, x: &[f64], v: &[f64]) -> Result<Vector, FieldError>

Exponential map at an arbitrary basepoint: step from x along the geodesic in direction v for ‖v‖ units of geodesic distance — x ⊕_κ exp_origin(v). Inverse of log_at by left-cancellation.

Source

pub fn geodesic( &self, x: &[f64], y: &[f64], t: f64, ) -> Result<Vector, FieldError>

The geodesic from x toward y at fraction t of the spherical distance: d(x, γ(t)) = t·d(x,y) exactly. γ(0) = x, γ(1) = y; t outside [0,1] continues along the same great circle — the geodesic wraps around the sphere.

Source

pub fn point_reflection( &self, x: &[f64], through: &[f64], ) -> Result<Vector, FieldError>

Geodesic point reflection through through: the point as far past through as x is before it — the symmetric-space involution s_c(x). through is the geodesic midpoint of x and its image, and the map is its own inverse: s_c(s_c(x)) = x.

Source

pub fn antipode(&self, x: &[f64]) -> Result<Vector, FieldError>

The true spherical antipode — the point maximally distant from x at d = π/√κ. Under stereographic projection the antipode map is inversion in the equator circle composed with negation: −x/(κ‖x‖²). It lands outside the cap (in the southern hemisphere), the genuine “far side.” The pole’s own antipode is the unrepresentable south pole at infinity — an error, not a clamp.

Source

fn validate_point(&self, x: &[f64]) -> Result<(), FieldError>

A valid chart point: right dimension, finite. Any such point names a sphere point in the stereographic chart — cap membership (contains) is a separate, stricter question.

Source

fn tangent_barycenter(&self, points: &[Vector]) -> Result<Vector, FieldError>

The tangent-space mean at the pole — the Euclidean average of the lifted log coordinates, mapped back. Exact for a single point and the right seed for frechet_mean; away from the pole it is only the barycenter’s first approximation, since the log map distorts with distance.

Source

pub fn frechet_mean(&self, points: &[Vector]) -> Result<Vector, FieldError>

The Fréchet mean: the point minimizing Σᵢ d²(m, xᵢ) — the true center of mass on the manifold, where the tangent-space mean is only its polar approximation. Computed by Karcher iteration: m ← exp_m(meanᵢ log_m(xᵢ)), steepest descent on ½Σd² (whose Riemannian gradient is −Σ log_m(xᵢ)). Positive curvature changes the guarantee, not the method: on the sphere the mean is unique whenever the points lie in an open hemisphere (Buss & Fillmore 2001) — exactly the cap the field operates in — and ½d² stays strictly convex while every pairwise distance stays under π/(2√κ). Seeded at the tangent barycenter, which is already the exact answer at the pole.
