1//! Neurochemical system — Genesis's affective engine.
2//!
3//! ## Modeling note
4//!
5//! This module is an **affective control substrate** — a compact
6//! 18-dimensional state space for control — not a concentration or
7//! pharmacokinetic model. The scalar-per-chemical abstraction is a
8//! computational analogue of neuromodulation, not a literal model of
9//! brain chemistry. It ignores compartments (synaptic cleft vs
10//! extrasynaptic vs plasma), receptor subtypes (except for the
11//! D1/D2 weighting applied to dopamine), cell-type specificity, and
12//! regional anatomy (a single "serotonin" scalar elides the dorsal
13//! vs median raphe projection differences). The coupling matrix and
14//! Hill-function gating are control-theoretic abstractions inspired
15//! by pharmacological dose-response curves, not a simulation of
16//! receptor-level kinetics. The "pharmacological reality" language
17//! used below refers to structural inspiration, not quantitative
18//! pharmacological accuracy.
19//!
20//! # What makes this novel
21//!
22//! Most computational affect models use independent dimensions
23//! (valence/arousal) or simple rule-based emotion systems. This module
24//! implements something structurally different:
25//!
26//! ## Coupled dynamics
27//!
28//! The 18 neurochemicals are not independent. A 18×18 coupling matrix
29//! defines how each chemical modulates the others' rates of change.
30//! Dopamine inhibits serotonin. Cortisol suppresses BDNF. GABA inhibits
31//! glutamate. Endorphins disinhibit dopamine. Orexin excites histamine.
32//! The system has emergent behaviour that cannot be predicted from
33//! any single chemical in isolation.
34//!
35//! ## Receptor adaptation
36//!
37//! Each chemical has a `receptor_sensitivity` field. The *effective*
38//! level — what consumers actually see — is `level × receptor_sensitivity`,
39//! not the raw concentration. Sustained high dopamine causes
40//! downregulation (tolerance). Sustained low serotonin causes
41//! upregulation (sensitization). This mirrors how real pharmacology
42//! works. Receptor-level dynamics are well established in computational
43//! psychiatry and biophysical modelling; we are not aware of another AI
44//! *control architecture* that integrates receptor adaptation into a
45//! closed-loop neuromodulatory substrate coupled to physical hardware
46//! in this form, but the individual mechanisms are not novel to
47//! neuroscience.
48//!
49//! ## Metaplasticity
50//!
51//! The coupling matrix itself is plastic. It lives in the mmap'd struct
52//! and can be modified by the dynamics engine over time. The brain
53//! doesn't just learn by changing weights — it learns by changing HOW
54//! it learns.
55//!
56//! ## BDNF-gated plasticity
57//!
58//! The system's ability to form new memories is gated by BDNF, which is
59//! suppressed by chronic cortisol. This captures, structurally, why
60//! chronic stress impairs learning — and why recovery (rising BDNF via
61//! serotonin, exercise analogues) restores it.
62//!
63//! ## Emergent phase transitions
64//!
65//! Mental states (flow, stress, drowsy, sleeping) emerge from the
66//! coupled dynamics rather than being manually switched. You don't set
67//! "flow mode" — flow emerges when dopamine, ACh, and NE are high while
68//! cortisol is low.

82/// Simple deterministic hash for the stochastic noise process.
83///
84/// Produces a pseudo-random u32 from a (seed, index) pair. This is
85/// NOT cryptographically secure — it's a fast hash for generating
86/// Ornstein-Uhlenbeck fluctuations. Uses splitmix64 mixing.
87#[inline]
88fn neuro_noise_hash(seed: u64, index: u64) -> u32 {
89    let mut z = seed.wrapping_add(index.wrapping_mul(0x9E3779B97F4A7C15));
90    z = (z ^ (z >> 30)).wrapping_mul(0xBF58476D1CE4E5B9);
91    z = (z ^ (z >> 27)).wrapping_mul(0x94D049BB133111EB);
92    z ^= z >> 31;
93    (z & 0xFFFFFFFF) as u32
94}

113/// Suprathreshold recruitment gain for coupling drive.
114///
115/// The coupling force from chemical j is driven by j's deviation
116/// from baseline. Linear-in-deviation coupling under-measures
117/// suprathreshold events: a strong release (impulse, stress
118/// surge, arousal burst) recruits extrasynaptic and
119/// volume-transmission receptors only once the synaptic envelope
120/// overflows, so the downstream influence of an *elevated*
121/// source is superlinear in its deviation (Hill-type
122/// cooperativity; Fuxe et al., 2010 on volume transmission).
123///
124/// Implementation: positive deviations are driven as
125/// `dev·(1 + GAIN·dev)` — at the resting wander (~±0.03) the
126/// multiplier is ~1.06, so near-baseline dynamics, the resting
127/// linearized spectrum, and the depletion death-spiral
128/// protections are essentially untouched; at an assay-scale
129/// deviation (0.30) it is ~1.6. Negative (depletion)
130/// deviations stay linear — they are already bounded by the
131/// -AUTORECEPTOR_THRESHOLD cap, and suppressed signaling does
132/// not super-recruit.
133///
134/// Calibration: sized so a suprathreshold probe moves a weakly
135/// coupled responder across the assay's 0.02 measurement floor
136/// (the B1 effective-connectivity measurement in
137/// notebooks/consciousness_correlates.ipynb). The causal assay
138/// showed linear drive produced mean 0.61 significant
139/// responders; with the diffuse tier at 0.10 and this gain,
140/// first-hop propagation roughly doubles.
141pub const SUPRATHRESHOLD_COUPLING_GAIN: f32 = 2.0;

163/// Bound on the arousal self-excitation term, in net-drive units.
164///
165/// The self-excitation used to be linear and unbounded:
166/// `(arousal - 0.5) * 2.05`. That is the textbook bistability
167/// condition — the local loop gain at `arousal = 0.5` is
168/// `WC_GAIN * 2.05 = 4.1`, and because the sigmoid contributes at
169/// most 0.5 of slope there, the total clears 1.0 by a 2.5% margin.
170///
171/// That margin is far too thin, and it made the readout a switch
172/// rather than a level. Solving the fixed-point equation showed the
173/// old form admitted *no* moderate wake state at all: for any
174/// `net_drive > 0` the attractor was ~0.85-0.90, for any
175/// `net_drive < 0` it was ~0.10-0.32, and the transition between
176/// them happened across a `net_drive` span of about 0.02. Genesis
177/// therefore locked near 0.9 while idle (measured: 0.900 within 100
178/// s, still 0.884 after four hours) and reported her resting state
179/// as "excited". Sustained arousal then drove the chronic-stress
180/// drain that pulled dopamine/serotonin/GABA progressively away from
181/// their genetic baselines.
182///
183/// The fix is to bound the term so it damps excursions instead of
184/// amplifying them:
185///
186/// ```text
187/// se(a) = WC_SELF_EXCITATION * tanh(WC_SELF_EXCITATION_SLOPE * (a - 0.5))
188/// ```
189///
190/// `tanh` caps the contribution at +/- `WC_SELF_EXCITATION`, so no
191/// amount of self-excitation can drive arousal into the sigmoid
192/// ceiling. Arousal becomes a graded function of chemistry: over a
193/// realistic `net_drive` range of -0.40..+0.14 it now spans roughly
194/// 0.19..0.63 instead of collapsing to 0.08 or 0.90.
195///
196/// The cost is that the Wilson-Cowan term no longer supplies
197/// hysteresis, and that is deliberate. Gradedness and bistability are
198/// mutually exclusive in a sigmoid form — meeting the bistability
199/// condition requires the loop gain above ~4, which is exactly what
200/// saturates the readout. Sleep-wake switching does not depend on
201/// this term: it is carried by the `MentalPhase` thresholds and by
202/// the sleep-wake network's stage hysteresis — a much stronger and
203/// better-placed source (the network's flip-flop decides the stage
204/// and the chemistry follows, rather than an arousal clamp forcing it).
205pub const WC_SELF_EXCITATION: f32 = 0.15;

207/// How sharply the bounded self-excitation engages around mid-arousal.
208/// With `WC_SELF_EXCITATION = 0.15` the local slope contribution is
209/// `AROUSAL_GAIN * WC_SELF_EXCITATION * WC_SELF_EXCITATION_SLOPE = 0.9`,
210/// i.e. deliberately below the 1.0 needed for bistability so the
211/// response stays monotone and graded.
212pub const WC_SELF_EXCITATION_SLOPE: f32 = 2.0;

214/// Acetylcholinesterase degradation rate for acetylcholine (ACh).
215///
216/// ACh is rapidly broken down in the synaptic cleft by
217/// acetylcholinesterase (AChE), one of the fastest enzymes in the
218/// body (turnover ~25,000 molecules/sec). This happens on a
219/// millisecond timescale — far faster than the homeostatic restoring
220/// force, which operates on a seconds-to-minutes timescale.
221///
222/// The degradation applies only to the EXCESS above the homeostatic
223/// baseline (not the absolute level). This preserves the biological
224/// intent — high ACh bursts decay rapidly — while allowing the
225/// homeostatic force to maintain ACh near baseline at rest. See the
226/// application site in `tick_with_params` for the full rationale.
227///
228/// Value 0.03 per 100 ms integration step (0.3/sec): an excess of
229/// 0.10 above baseline decays by 3% per step, reaching baseline in
230/// ~3.5 steps (~0.35 seconds) without homeostatic help. This is compressed from
231/// the biological millisecond timescale but preserves the key
232/// property: ACh is the fastest-degrading neurotransmitter in the
233/// model.
234pub const ACH_DEGRADATION_RATE: f32 = 0.03;

236/// Cortisol metabolic clearance rate (hepatic 11β-HSD degradation).
237///
238/// Cortisol is cleared from the bloodstream by the liver via
239/// 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase enzymes. In biology, cortisol
240/// has a half-life of ~60–90 minutes with first-order kinetics
241/// (Basic & Clinical Pharmacology, 13th Ed.).
242///
243/// The clearance here is nonlinear and phenomenological — proportional
244/// to the excess above baseline, applied in the exact exponential form
245/// `level *= exp(-rate * excess * dt / DT)`:
246///
247/// ```text
248/// effective_rate = CORTISOL_CLEARANCE_RATE * max(0, level - baseline) / DT
249/// ```
250///
251/// At 0.08 per tick-scale the effective decay is fast when cortisol is
252/// elevated but vanishes at baseline. This captures the upregulation of
253/// 11β-HSD2 under sustained high cortisol and holds the equilibrium at
254/// the measured reachable envelope (~0.06 under sustained drive, well
255/// below the 0.80 hard clamp), without the level ever pinning at the
256/// clamp. The linear metabolic term ([`CORTISOL_METABOLIC_RATE`])
257/// dominates near baseline, where this term vanishes, and sets the
258/// biologically measured low-level tail.
259///
260/// (Tomlinson et al., 2004; Hellhammer et al., 2009; Nature Communications 2024 on 11β-HSD)
261pub const CORTISOL_CLEARANCE_RATE: f32 = 0.08;

263/// First-order metabolic leak for cortisol, per second.
264///
265/// Cortisol's measured plasma half-life is ~66 min at normal hormone

276/// Measured plasma CRH clearance is biexponential: human CRH

287// ─── Circulating endocrine pools ─────────────────────────────
288//
289// Epinephrine, oxytocin, β-endorphin, and vasopressin circulate in
290// plasma and CSF on minute-scale turnover — not the synaptic
291// timescale the generic neural spring encodes (~seconds). Each is
292// modeled as a first-order secretion/clearance pool,
293//
294//     dL/dt = k·(L_basal − L) + release drives + coupling modulators
295//
296// which is the exact reduced form of a pool with secretion rate S and
297// first-order clearance k (equilibrium L_basal = S/k). The neural
298// spring is skipped for them: it would restore a released bolus in
299// ~seconds, ~100× faster than their measured plasma half-lives, so
300// endocrine signals would carry no memory. Coupling inputs and
301// impulses still act — they are the release modulators — attenuated
302// onto the slow pool by HORMONE_COUPLING_GAIN so a persistent neural
303// push settles at a bounded deviation rather than pinning the clamp.

461/// Measured plasma clearance for the circulating endocrine chemicals,
462/// or `None` for everything else (neural transmitters keep the generic
463/// spring; adenosine, cortisol, CRH, melatonin, and the network-driven
464/// monoamines have their own mechanisms).
465const fn circulating_hormone_clearance(idx: usize) -> Option<f32> {
466    if idx == NeurochemicalId::Epinephrine as usize {
467        Some(EPINEPHRINE_METABOLIC_RATE)
468    } else if idx == NeurochemicalId::Oxytocin as usize {
469        Some(OXYTOCIN_METABOLIC_RATE)
470    } else if idx == NeurochemicalId::Endorphin as usize {
471        Some(ENDORPHIN_METABOLIC_RATE)
472    } else if idx == NeurochemicalId::Vasopressin as usize {
473        Some(VASOPRESSIN_METABOLIC_RATE)
474    } else if idx == NeurochemicalId::Cortisol as usize {
475        // Cortisol is a circulating steroid — it belongs to this
476        // pool, not to the neural velocity update. The velocity
477        // system's homeostatic force pulled its level toward a
478        // baseline of literal 0.0, which crushed the basal HPA
479        // secretion to ~1e-5: an absent hormone, not a resting one.
480        // In this pool its level integrates ACTH drive, impulses,
481        // and the measured first-order clearance — so basal tone
482        // survives. The nonlinear CORTISOL_CLEARANCE_RATE term
483        // remains applied in the HPA section.
484        Some(CORTISOL_METABOLIC_RATE)
485    } else {
486        None
487    }
488}

520/// `pub(crate)` because `daemon::consolidation::compute_compact_tag`
521/// re-derives valence from stored emotional tags and must use the same
522/// scaling.
523#[inline]
524pub(crate) fn cortisol_stress_fraction(cort: f32) -> f32 {
525    crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(
526        (cort - CORTISOL_RESTING_LEVEL) / (CORTISOL_PEAK_LEVEL - CORTISOL_RESTING_LEVEL),
527        0.0,
528    )
529}

532/// system never sees literal zero — the substrate's 0 means a
533/// quiescent HPA axis, physiologically equivalent to the circadian
534/// trough (~CORTISOL_RESTING_LEVEL). Occupancy below the floor is
535/// computed as resting, not adrenalectomy — the substrate has no
536/// adrenalectomy state, and the envelope's left edge is the trough.

538fn basal_cortisol(cort: f32) -> f32 {
539    cort.max(CORTISOL_RESTING_LEVEL)

542/// Mineralocorticoid-receptor occupancy at an absolute cortisol
543/// level. MRs bind glucocorticoids with ~10× higher affinity than
544/// GRs and are already >90% occupied at circadian baseline
545/// (Reul & de Kloet, 1985), so Kd sits an order of magnitude below
546/// the resting level of ~0.02.
547#[inline]
548pub(crate) fn mr_occupancy(cort: f32) -> f32 {
549    let c = basal_cortisol(cort);
550    c / (c + 0.002)
551}

553/// Glucocorticoid-receptor occupancy at an absolute cortisol level.
554/// Measured occupancy is ~10% at the circadian trough and ~67–74%
555/// at the stress/circadian peak (Reul & de Kloet, 1985) — too steep
556/// a transition for single-site binding, so this uses a Hill
557/// coefficient of 2 (GRs signal as dimers): c²/(c² + 0.06²) gives
558/// ≈0.10 at rest and ≈0.64 at the 0.08 peak.
559#[inline]
560pub(crate) fn gr_occupancy(cort: f32) -> f32 {
561    let c = basal_cortisol(cort);
562    let c2 = c * c;
563    c2 / (c2 + 0.0036)
564}

566/// Maximum of `mr × (1 − gr)` over the cortisol range — reached at
567/// the circadian trough, ≈0.83. Used to normalize the facilitation
568/// inverted-U to ≈1 at its optimum.

571/// Corticosteroid support of hippocampal LTP — an inverted-U against
572/// absolute cortisol (Diamond et al., 1992: primed-burst potentiation
573/// peaked at intermediate corticosterone and fell on both sides).
574/// De Kloet et al. (1999) recast the curve as the MR/GR occupancy
575/// ratio: plasticity is best when MRs are saturated and GRs only
576/// partly occupied, and degrades as GR-dominated stress levels
577/// recruit the low-affinity receptors. Resting cortisol sits at the
578/// optimum; supra-peak levels suppress asymptotically — graded,
579/// never a hard cutoff.

581pub(crate) fn corticosteroid_ltp_facilitation(cort: f32) -> f32 {
582    mr_occupancy(cort) * (1.0 - gr_occupancy(cort)) / LTP_FACILITATION_PEAK

586/// inverted-U dose response (2000): post-training glucocorticoids at
587/// moderate stress doses enhance consolidation, while low doses
588/// contribute nothing and supra-peak doses lose the effect. Shaped
589/// as x·e^{1−x} over the envelope fraction so it rises from ≈0 at

793/// Autoreceptor self-inhibition threshold.
794///
795/// Autoreceptors (e.g., 5-HT1A for serotonin, D2 for dopamine,
796/// alpha-2A for norepinephrine, GABA_B for GABA, M2 for acetylcholine)
797/// provide negative feedback that strengthens when neurotransmitter
798/// levels deviate far from baseline. Near baseline (|deviation| ≤
799/// this threshold), autoreceptor occupancy is low and the restoring
800/// force is purely the standard homeostatic pull. Beyond this
801/// threshold, autoreceptor-mediated self-inhibition grows
802/// quadratically, preventing chemicals from pinning at the 0.0/1.0
803/// clamps.
804///
805/// Set to 0.15 so that moderate deviations needed for emergent phases
806/// (NE=0.60 for Stress, DA=0.65 for Flow — deviations of ~0.20-0.25)
807/// are only mildly affected, while large deviations (0.35+) engage
808/// strong self-inhibition.
809pub const AUTORECEPTOR_THRESHOLD: f32 = 0.15;

811/// Autoreceptor self-inhibition gain.
812///
813/// Beyond [`AUTORECEPTOR_THRESHOLD`], the homeostatic restoring force
814/// is multiplied by `(1 + AUTORECEPTOR_GAIN * excess²)`, where
815/// `excess = |deviation| - threshold`. This creates a quadratic
816/// "soft wall" at extremes, modeling the disproportionate increase in
817/// autoreceptor-mediated inhibition when neurotransmitter levels
818/// deviate far from set-points.
819///
820/// This is the permanent, biologically grounded nonlinear stabilizer
821/// for the coupled neurochemical system. The coupling matrix alone has
822/// a spectral radius ~7.6× too large for global linear stability, but
823/// emergent phases require strong coupling. Autoreceptors resolve this
824/// by leaving near-baseline dynamics (phases) linear while providing
825/// the strong self-inhibition that prevents extreme clamping.
826///
827/// See the homeostatic force computation in `tick_with_params` for
828/// the full rationale and calibration.
829pub const AUTORECEPTOR_GAIN: f32 = 200.0;

831/// Functional rescue: receptor turnover and effective-deviation scaling.
832///
833/// Two mechanisms prevent permanent receptor burnout traps:
834///
835/// 1. **Effective-deviation scaling**: receptor downregulation is
836///    driven by receptor activation (effective signaling), not by
837///    raw ligand concentration. The deviation used for downregulation
838///    is scaled by receptor health: `effective_deviation = raw_deviation
839///    × sensitivity × desensitization × internalization`. When
840///    receptors are burnt out, effective deviation is small, so
841///    downregulation is weak. This is biologically correct — receptor
842///    downregulation is a response to overstimulation, and burnt-out
843///    receptors aren't being overstimulated.
844///
845/// 2. **Receptor turnover**: a slow background recovery toward 1.0
846///    that models the continuous receptor replacement/recycling that
847///    happens in biology (receptor half-life is days to weeks).
848///    This is always active but normally overwhelmed by deviation-gated
849///    downregulation. When downregulation is weak (low effective
850///    deviation due to burnout), turnover slowly restores receptor
851///    function.
852///
853///    Each factor's turnover is proportional to its adaptation rate,
854///    ordered by the biological cascade (5.0×, 1.0×, 0.2× for
855///    desensitization, internalization, sensitivity respectively —
856///    dephosphorylation is fast, membrane recycling intermediate,
857///    receptor synthesis slow). This ensures that:
858///    - In normal overstimulation: desensitization carries the acute
859///      adaptation on the measured minutes timescale, and recovers
860///      quickly once the drive drops.
861///    - In extreme burnout: health-scaled downregulation weakens as
862///      effective deviation collapses, letting turnover restore
863///      function; under sustained strong drive the factors correctly
864///      stay downregulated (chronic agonist → chronic tolerance).
865///
866/// These mechanisms are always active — no thresholds, no recovery
867/// zones, no limit cycles. The system smoothly transitions between
868/// normal adaptation and recovery as receptor health and deviation
869/// change.
870pub const RECEPTOR_TURNOVER_RATE: f32 = 0.0001;

1080    /// Multiplier for the global receptor adaptation rate.
1081    ///
1082    /// Different neuromodulator systems adapt at different speeds:
1083    /// dopaminergic and cholinergic receptors traffic rapidly; cortisol
1084    /// (genomic) and BDNF/TrkB (protein expression) adapt much more
1085    /// slowly. This multiplier scales the base `adaptation_rate` for
1086    /// the three receptor-adaptation mechanisms in each tick.

1088    /// Values are conservative (within [0.4, 1.2]) so the dynamics
1089    /// remain stable while still capturing the real kinetic spread
1090    /// across receptor systems.

1092        match self {
1093            Self::Dopamine => 1.2,       // D2/D3 receptors traffic rapidly
1094            Self::Serotonin => 0.9,      // 5-HT1A/2A: moderate trafficking
1095            Self::Norepinephrine => 1.1, // β-AR desensitizes quickly
1096            Self::Acetylcholine => 1.2,  // nicotinic receptors desensitize in ms
1097            Self::GABA => 0.9,           // GABA-A receptor turnover is moderate
1098            Self::Glutamate => 1.0,      // AMPA/NMDA: baseline reference
1099            Self::Cortisol => 0.5,       // GR/MR effects are genomic and slow
1100            Self::Oxytocin => 0.7,       // OTR trafficking is moderate-slow
1101            Self::Endorphin => 0.9,      // MOR internalization is moderate
1102            Self::Histamine => 1.0,
1103            // The somnogenic adenosine receptor is A1, and A1 is the
1104            // adenosine subtype that *resists* downregulation: "the
1105            // A1Rs are not (readily) phosphorylated and internalize
1106            // slowly, showing a typical half-life of several hours,
1107            // whereas the A2AR and A2BR undergo much faster
1108            // downregulation, usually shorter than 1 h" (Klaasse et
1109            // al. 2008, Pharmacol Rev / PMC2245999). A3 is faster
1110            // still, "often a matter of minutes".
1111            //
1112            // Modelling A1 as average-trafficking (it previously shared
1113            // the 1.0 "baseline reference" with AMPA/NMDA) let chronic
1114            // sleep pressure collapse the receptor factors: measured
1115            // over 48 h, raw adenosine sat at 0.90 while effective
1116            // level fell to 0.126 with sensitivity pinned at its 0.5
1117            // floor. Because the sleep gate reads a blend of effective
1118            // and raw adenosine, that collapse dragged the gate value
1119            // to 0.513 — under the 0.65 stay threshold — making sleep
1120            // unrecoverable and latching the phase machine into a
1121            // self-sustaining exhaustion loop (80,871 sleep bouts in
1122            // 48 h, mean duration one tick).
1123            //
1124            // 0.3 encodes A1's resistance: several-hour internalization
1125            // half-life against A2A/A2B's sub-hour one.
1126            Self::Adenosine => 0.3,
1127            Self::BDNF => 0.3, // TrkB expression and BDNF synthesis are slow
1128            Self::Endocannabinoid => 1.0,
1129            Self::Vasopressin => 0.6, // V1a/V1b trafficking is slow
1130            Self::CRH => 0.5,         // CRHR1/2 signaling and trafficking are slow
1131            Self::Orexin => 0.9,
1132            Self::Epinephrine => 1.1, // similar to NE
1133            Self::Melatonin => 0.4,   // MT1/MT2 are slow, hormone-mediated

1161/// A single neurochemical's dynamic state.
1162///
1163/// ## Layout (56 bytes)
1164/// ```text
1165/// offset  field                  type      notes
1166/// ------  -----                  ----      -----
1167///   0     last_shift_at          u64       ms timestamp of last significant shift
1168///   8     level                  f32       current concentration [0.0, 1.0]
1169///  12     baseline               f32       homeostatic set-point (adapts slowly)
1170///  16     receptor_sensitivity   f32       how strongly effects are felt [0.5, 2.0]
1171///  20     velocity               f32       rate of change (momentum)
1172///  24     receptor_subtypes      [f32; 2]  subtype balance (D1/D2, 5-HT1A/5-HT2A)
1173///  32     desensitization_factor f32       fast phosphorylation-based adaptation
1174///  36     internalization_factor f32       slow membrane-removal adaptation
1175///  40     tonic_level            f32       background sustained level (slow-changing)
1176///  44     phasic_level           f32       burst-firing transient (fast-decaying)
1177///  48     vesicular_pool         f32       vesicle pool fullness [0.0, 1.0]
1178///  52     id                     u8        NeurochemicalId discriminant
1179///  53     _pad                   [u8; 3]   alignment to 56
1180/// ```
1181#[repr(C)]
1182#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug)]
1183pub struct Neurochemical {
1184    /// Milliseconds-timestamp of the last significant level shift.
1185    pub last_shift_at: u64,
1186    /// Current concentration, in `[0.0, 1.0]`.
1187    pub level: f32,
1188    /// Homeostatic set-point (adapts slowly).
1189    pub baseline: f32,
1190    /// Receptor sensitivity — how strongly effects are felt, in `[0.5, 2.0]`.
1191    pub receptor_sensitivity: f32,
1192    /// Rate of change (momentum) of the level.
1193    pub velocity: f32,
1194    /// Receptor subtype balance [subtype_a, subtype_b].
1195    /// For dopamine: [D1 (excitatory), D2 (inhibitory)]
1196    /// For serotonin: [5-HT1A (anxiolytic), 5-HT2A (hallucinogenic)]
1197    /// For orexin: [OX1R (excitatory), OX2R (wake-promoting)]
1198    /// For epinephrine: [alpha-AR, beta-AR]
1199    /// For others: [1.0, 1.0] (no subtype differentiation)
1200    pub receptor_subtypes: [f32; 2],
1201    /// Fast desensitization factor (phosphorylation-based, reversible in
1202    /// minutes). Range [0.2, 1.0] where 1.0 = no desensitization, 0.2 =
1203    /// maximally desensitized (~80% loss, per MOR/β2AR literature).
1204    /// Responds quickly to high levels.
1205    pub desensitization_factor: f32,
1206    /// Slow internalization factor (membrane removal, reversible in hours).
1207    /// Range [0.45, 1.0] where 1.0 = no internalization, 0.45 = maximally
1208    /// internalized (~55% loss, per β2AR/MOR literature). Responds to
1209    /// sustained high levels.
1210    pub internalization_factor: f32,
1211    /// Tonic (background, sustained) level. Changes slowly, representing
1212    /// baseline firing rate. For dopamine, tonic level activates
1213    /// high-affinity D2 receptors (mood maintenance). For serotonin,
1214    /// tonic level activates high-affinity 5-HT1A receptors (anxiolytic).
1215    /// Finite but not range-limited. Initialized to the default baseline.
1216    pub tonic_level: f32,
1217    /// Phasic (burst-firing, transient) level. Decays quickly (seconds),
1218    /// representing burst firing events. For dopamine, phasic bursts
1219    /// activate low-affinity D1 receptors (reward signaling). For
1220    /// serotonin, phasic bursts activate low-affinity 5-HT2A receptors
1221    /// (excitatory). Finite but not range-limited. Initialized to 0.0.
1222    pub phasic_level: f32,
1223    /// Vesicular pool fullness. Represents the readily releasable
1224    /// vesicle pool. On impulse (rapid level increase), the pool is
1225    /// depleted. When the pool is low, impulses have reduced effect
1226    /// (short-term synaptic depression). Synthesis and reuptake slowly
1227    /// replenish it; the value is finite but not range-limited.
1228    pub vesicular_pool: f32,
1229    /// `NeurochemicalId` discriminant identifying which chemical this is.
1230    pub id: u8,
1231    /// Alignment padding to reach 56 bytes.
1232    pub _pad: [u8; 3],
1233}

1235impl Neurochemical {
1236    /// Create a neurochemical at its default baseline, zero velocity,
1237    /// normal receptor sensitivity.
1238    pub fn new(id: NeurochemicalId, now_ms: u64) -> Self {
1239        Self {
1240            last_shift_at: now_ms,
1241            level: id.default_baseline(),
1242            baseline: id.default_baseline(),
1243            receptor_sensitivity: id.default_sensitivity(),
1244            velocity: 0.0,
1245            receptor_subtypes: id.default_receptor_subtypes(),
1246            desensitization_factor: 1.0,
1247            internalization_factor: 1.0,
1248            tonic_level: id.default_baseline(),
1249            phasic_level: 0.0,
1250            vesicular_pool: 1.0,
1251            id: id as u8,
1252            _pad: [0; 3],
1253        }
1254    }
1255
1256    /// The **effective level** — what consumers of neurochemical state
1257    /// actually see. This is `level × receptor_sensitivity ×
1258    /// desensitization_factor × internalization_factor`. The result is
1259    /// finite but not range-limited.
1260    ///
1261    /// This is the key insight from pharmacology: a chemical can be at
1262    /// high concentration but have low effect (tolerance/downregulation)
1263    /// or low concentration but high effect (sensitization/upregulation).
1264    ///
1265    /// The effective level now incorporates two separate receptor
1266    /// adaptation mechanisms:
1267    /// - **Desensitization** (fast, phosphorylation-based): reduces effect
1268    ///   within minutes of high stimulation
1269    /// - **Internalization** (slow, membrane removal): reduces effect over
1270    ///   hours of sustained high stimulation
1271    ///
1272    /// Both factors are in [0.2, 1.0] (desensitization) and [0.45, 1.0]
1273    /// (internalization), and multiply together with
1274    /// receptor_sensitivity [0.5, 2.0] to produce the final effective
1275    /// level.
1276    pub fn effective_level(&self) -> f32 {
1277        let raw = self.level
1278            * self.receptor_sensitivity
1279            * self.desensitization_factor
1280            * self.internalization_factor;
1281        crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(raw, 0.0)
1282    }
1283
1284    /// The **subtype-weighted effective level** — considers receptor
1285    /// subtype balance. For dopamine, D1 (excitatory) and D2 (inhibitory)
1286    /// have opposing effects. For serotonin, 5-HT1A (anxiolytic) and
1287    /// 5-HT2A (hallucinogenic) have different downstream effects.
1288    ///
1289    /// The subtype balance modifies the effective level: when subtype_a
1290    /// dominates, the "excitatory/anxiolytic" pathway is emphasized;
1291    /// when subtype_b dominates, the "inhibitory/hallucinogenic"
1292    /// pathway is emphasized. The net effect is a weighted combination.
1293    pub fn subtype_effective_level(&self) -> f32 {
1294        let base = self.effective_level();
1295        let id = NeurochemicalId::from_u8(self.id);
1296        match id {
1297            NeurochemicalId::Dopamine => {
1298                // D1 (excitatory) increases effective signaling,
1299                // D2 (inhibitory) decreases it. The net effect is
1300                // weighted by subtype balance.
1301                //
1302                // The formula `base * (d1 - d2) / total * 2.0 + base`
1303                // carries the full subtype-weighted signal; only
1304                // non-finite results fall back to the finite base.
1305                let d1 = self.receptor_subtypes[0];
1306                let d2 = self.receptor_subtypes[1];
1307                let total = d1 + d2;
1308                if total > 0.0 {
1309                    let raw = base * (d1 - d2) / total * 2.0 + base;
1310                    crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(raw, base)
1311                } else {
1312                    base
1313                }
1314            }
1315            NeurochemicalId::Serotonin => {
1316                // 5-HT1A (anxiolytic) promotes calm signaling,
1317                // 5-HT2A (hallucinogenic) promotes excitatory signaling.
1318                // The net effect is neutral at a 50/50 balance, biased
1319                // up or down by the relative receptor weights.
1320                let ht1a = self.receptor_subtypes[0];
1321                let ht2a = self.receptor_subtypes[1];
1322                let total = ht1a + ht2a;
1323                if total > 0.0 {
1324                    let balance = (ht1a - ht2a) / total;
1325                    crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(base * (1.0 + 0.3 * balance), base)
1326                } else {
1327                    base
1328                }
1329            }
1330            _ => base,
1331        }
1332    }
1333
1334    /// Apply an impulse (external event) to the level.
1335    ///
1336    /// `magnitude` is in normalised units; positive increases, negative
1337    /// decreases. Velocity is updated to model momentum.
1338    ///
1339    /// # Vesicular pool depletion
1340    ///
1341    /// On a positive impulse (release event), the vesicular pool is
1342    /// depleted proportional to the magnitude. When the pool is low,
1343    /// the impulse effect is reduced (short-term synaptic depression).
1344    /// This prevents infinite sustained high levels — the system has
1345    /// a finite neurotransmitter supply that must be replenished by
1346    /// synthesis (slow) and reuptake (moderate).
1347    ///
1348    /// # Phasic burst
1349    ///
1350    /// Positive impulses also trigger a phasic burst — a transient
1351    /// increase in phasic_level that decays quickly. This models
1352    /// burst firing in dopaminergic and serotonergic neurons, where
1353    /// salient events cause transient high-frequency firing that
1354    /// activates low-affinity receptors (D1, 5-HT2A) distinct from
1355    /// the tonic baseline that activates high-affinity receptors
1356    /// (D2, 5-HT1A).
1357    pub fn apply_impulse(&mut self, magnitude: f32, now_ms: u64) {
1358        // Defense-in-depth: sanitize the magnitude before it touches
1359        // any chemical field. Even though IPC entry points now reject
1360        // NaN, `apply_impulse` is also called from:
1361        // - `active_inference::apply_inference_feedback` (impulses
1362        //   computed from the transition matrix, which may be
1363        //   corrupted)
1364        // - `dyadic_model::DyadicAffectModel::update` (impulses
1365        //   computed from user affect, which may be NaN if the
1366        //   dyadic model's internal state was corrupted)
1367        // - `interoception::Interoceptor::neuro_impulses` (computed
1368        //   from hardware sensors — unlikely but not impossible)
1369        //
1370        // A NaN magnitude here would make `level` and `velocity` NaN,
1371        // which would propagate through the coupling matrix to all 18
1372        // chemicals in the next tick. Replacing NaN with 0.0 produces
1373        // a no-op impulse — the chemical is unchanged, which is the
1374        // safest possible behavior for an invalid input.
1375        let magnitude = crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(magnitude, 0.0);
1376
1377        // Vesicular pool: positive impulses deplete the pool, reducing
1378        // the effective magnitude when the pool is low (synaptic depression).
1379        // Deliver, then deplete by what was actually delivered.
1380        //
1381        // The order matters and was previously wrong in a way that
1382        // inverted the dose-response relationship. Depletion was
1383        // charged on the *requested* dose before delivery, so an
1384        // impulse that the pool could not honour still emptied it. The
1385        // system latched off: once the pool reached zero, every further
1386        // impulse was refused *and* kept depleting it, so recovery was
1387        // the only way out and sustained stimulation was impossible.
1388        //
1389        // Measured directly, driving CRH harder produced less of it:
1390        //
1391        //   drive/tick   CRH effective   vesicular pool
1392        //     0.0005         0.204            1.000
1393        //     0.0020         0.204            0.900
1394        //     0.0040         0.204            0.301
1395        //     0.0080         0.049            0.001   <- 4x drive, 4x less
1396        //     0.0320         0.078            0.001
1397        //
1398        // CRH carries the stress axis, so a stress response could not
1399        // exceed a ceiling of about 0.004/tick and beyond that reversed
1400        // — sustained alarm produced *less* cortisol than mild stress,
1401        // and the Stress phase (gated at cortisol 0.65) was
1402        // unreachable under any drive. Depletion is now charged on
1403        // delivered quantity, which is bounded by the pool, so an
1404        // empty pool stops depleting and can recover.
1405        let effective_magnitude = if magnitude > 0.0 {
1406            // Non-finite pool state cannot propagate into the delivered
1407            // impulse, but finite pool values carry through unbounded.
1408            let pool_factor = crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(self.vesicular_pool, 0.0);
1409            let delivered = magnitude * pool_factor;
1410            self.vesicular_pool = crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(
1411                self.vesicular_pool - delivered * VESICULAR_DEPLETION,
1412                0.0,
1413            );
1414            delivered
1415        } else {
1416            magnitude
1417        };
1418
1419        // Velocity is a dynamics state variable, not a signal — it
1420        // gets a stability bound, not a range clamp on the signal.
1421        // Without this, a large impulse (even after IPC-level
1422        // magnitude checks) sets velocity to an enormous value that
1423        // decays geometrically and keeps the chemical saturated for
1424        // hundreds of ticks — a local DoS that distorts the whole
1425        // neurochemical state. [-1, 1] is generous: normal impulses
1426        // produce velocities orders of magnitude smaller.
1427        self.velocity = crate::state::sanitize::finite_clamp(
1428            crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(self.velocity + effective_magnitude * 0.3, 0.0),
1429            -1.0,
1430            1.0,
1431        );
1432        self.level = crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(self.level + effective_magnitude, 0.0);
1433
1434        // Phasic burst: positive impulses trigger a transient burst
1435        // that activates low-affinity receptors (D1 for DA, 5-HT2A for SRT)
1436        if effective_magnitude > 0.0 {
1437            let next_phasic = self.phasic_level + effective_magnitude * 0.5;
1438            self.phasic_level = crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(next_phasic, 0.0);
1439        }
1440
1441        if magnitude.abs() > 0.05 {
1442            self.last_shift_at = now_ms;
1443        }
1444    }
1445}

1451/// The 18×18 neurochemical coupling matrix.
1452///
1453/// `coupling_matrix[i][j]` defines how chemical j influences chemical i's
1454/// rate of change. Positive = excitatory, negative = inhibitory, zero =
1455/// no direct coupling. The diagonal is zero (self-dynamics are handled
1456/// by homeostatic drift, not self-coupling).
1457///
1458/// ## Grounding in real neuroscience
1459///
1460/// These values are derived from known neurotransmitter interactions:
1461/// - Serotonin inhibits dopamine (mesolimbic pathway)
1462/// - Cortisol suppresses BDNF and serotonin (stress-damage mechanism)
1463/// - GABA inhibits glutamate (primary inhibitory/excitatory balance)
1464/// - Adenosine's sleep pressure acts via level accumulation/clearance, not direct coupling
1465/// - Endorphins disinhibit dopamine (runner's high mechanism)
1466/// - Oxytocin dampens cortisol (social bonding reduces stress)
1467/// - BDNF is promoted by serotonin (why SSRIs help depression)
1468/// - Endocannabinoids retrogradely inhibit GABA and glutamate
1469/// - Vasopressin synergizes with oxytocin and stimulates cortisol
1470/// - CRH triggers cortisol release (HPA axis upstream)
1471/// - Cortisol negatively feeds back to CRH
1472/// - Orexin mutually excites histamine and promotes arousal (NE, DA, ACh)
1473/// - Orexin is inhibited by adenosine and GABA (sleep pressure)
1474/// - Epinephrine is stimulated by sympathetic activity (NE, ACh, CRH)
1475/// - Epinephrine promotes cardiovascular arousal (cortisol, NE)
1476/// - Melatonin inhibits dopamine (circadian mood modulation)
1477/// - Melatonin inhibits histamine (sleep promotion via wake-center suppression)
1478/// - Melatonin promotes GABA (sleep facilitation)
1479/// - Melatonin inhibits orexin (sleep-wake switch toward sleep)
1480///
1481/// ## Metaplasticity
1482///
1483/// The coupling matrix is not static. It lives in the mmap'd state and
1484/// can be modified by the dynamics engine over time. This represents
1485/// neurochemical-level learning — the brain rewiring its own
1486/// neurotransmitter interactions based on experience.
1487pub type CouplingMatrix = [[f32; NEUROCHEMICAL_COUNT]; NEUROCHEMICAL_COUNT];

1489/// The default coupling matrix, grounded in neuroscience literature.
1490///
1491/// Index order: DA, SRT, NE, ACh, GABA, GLU, CORT, OXY, END, HIST, ADN,
1492/// BDNF, ECB, VP, CRH, OX (orexin), EPI (epinephrine), MEL (melatonin)
1493///
1494/// ## Research grounding
1495///
1496/// The coupling strengths are grounded in the neuroscience literature on
1497/// neuromodulatory volume transmission (Özçete et al., Molecular
1498/// Psychiatry, 2024) and computational models of neurotransmitter
1499/// dynamics (Deco et al., PNAS, 2021; Hellyer et al., 2025).
1500///
1501/// Key principles:
1502/// - **Monoamines (DA, SRT, NE, ACh) interact weakly** (0.10) through
1503///   volume transmission — they diffuse broadly and have modulatory,
1504///   not strong, effects on each other. The diffuse tier was originally
1505///   calibrated at 0.05, which sat under the measurable-propagation
1506///   floor: in the causal assay (eval/assay.py) a suprathreshold impulse
1507///   moved its strongest coupled responder by ~0.01-0.02 on average
1508///   (0.61 responders ≥0.02), so the recurrent web was present in
1509///   structure but below threshold in effect. The tier is doubled to
1510///   0.10 — effective coupling 0.015 at COUPLING_SCALE=0.15 — so diffuse
1511///   modulatory influence actually propagates while staying well below
1512///   the homeostatic rate of 0.04.
1513/// - **GLU↔GABA is the strong E/I axis** (-0.30 GABA→GLU, +0.10 GLU→GABA).
1514///   This asymmetric negative feedback loop stabilizes excitation-inhibition
1515///   balance: GLU rises → drives GABA up (feedback inhibition) → GABA
1516///   suppresses GLU. This is the primary dynamic axis of the brain.
1517/// - **Cortisol and CRH have NO positive couplings from arousal chemicals.**
1518///   They are produced only by the HPA cascade (maturation-gated). The
1519///   coupling matrix only has suppressive feedback (SRT, GABA, OXY, END,
1520///   BDNF, ECB → CORT/CRH negative).
1521/// - **Peptides (OXY, END, VP) interact weakly** (0.10) — they are potent
1522///   but very low concentration (~10 pM oxytocin, ~5-15 fmol/mL endorphin).
1523/// - **BDNF is supported by monoamines** (especially SRT, +0.10, the
1524///   antidepressant mechanism) **and suppressed by cortisol** (-0.20).
1525/// - **All positive feedback loops are weak** (0.10) to prevent runaway
1526///   dynamics. With COUPLING_SCALE=0.15, effective coupling is 0.015,
1527///   well below the homeostatic rate of 0.04.

1904    /// # Adenosine is one-way
1905    ///
1906    /// Adenosine is Process S: its level is the organism's memory of
1907    /// time spent awake, and only the dedicated mechanism may reduce

1974    /// desensitization × internalization), including phasic boosts for
1975    /// dopamine and serotonin and allosteric modulation for GABA.

1977    /// This must produce the same value as
1978    /// `self.effective_levels[id as usize]` after
1979    /// [`NeurochemicalVector::recompute_derived`](Self::recompute_derived) has been called.

1981    /// causing [`sync_neurochemistry_to_state`](super::core_state::GenesisCoreState::sync_neurochemistry_to_state)
1982    /// — which drives memory gating weights — to miss reward burst
1983    /// (DA phasic → D1) and excitatory serotonin (SRT phasic → 5-HT2A)
1984    /// signals that the coupling matrix and arousal/valence equations
1985    /// already saw via `effective_levels[]`.

1988            Some(c) => c,
1989            None => return 0.0,
1990        };

1993            NeurochemicalId::GABA => {
1994                // Allosteric modulation: benzodiazepine-like enhancement
1995                // of GABA-A sensitivity. When gaba_a_allosteric > 0,
1996                // GABA's effective level is multiplied by (1 + allosteric * 0.5).

1998            }

2001                // receptors, adding signaling beyond the tonic baseline.

2004            }
2005            NeurochemicalId::Serotonin => {
2006                // Phasic burst activates low-affinity 5-HT2A (excitatory)
2007                // receptors, adding signaling beyond the tonic baseline.
2008                let ht2a_weight = chem.receptor_subtypes[1];
2009                crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(
2010                    base + chem.phasic_level * ht2a_weight * 0.2,
2011                    base,
2012                )
2013            }
2014            _ => base,
2015        }

2054    /// Compute the melatonin secretion factor [0.0, 1.0] from the
2055    /// circadian phase. Melatonin is high during the biological night,
2056    /// with a sharp evening onset, a nocturnal plateau, and a sharp
2057    /// morning offset (Arendt, 1998; Benloucif et al., 2005).
2058    ///
2059    /// The new profile replaces the old symmetric Gaussian with a
2060    /// piecewise smoothstep model:
2061    /// - Day (0.30–0.70): melatonin is essentially 0.
2062    /// - Evening onset (0.70–0.85): melatonin rises from 0 to 1.
2063    /// - Biological night (0.85–0.15, wrap): melatonin stays at 1.
2064    /// - Morning offset (0.15–0.30): melatonin falls from 1 to 0.
2065    ///
2066    /// Example values:
2067    /// - Phase 0.0 (midnight): 1.0
2068    /// - Phase 0.10 (02:24): 1.0
2069    /// - Phase 0.20 (04:48): ~0.74
2070    /// - Phase 0.25 (06:00): ~0.26
2071    /// - Phase 0.30 (07:12): 0.0
2072    /// - Phase 0.70 (16:48): 0.0
2073    /// - Phase 0.80 (19:12): ~0.26
2074    /// - Phase 0.90 (21:36): ~0.74
2075    pub fn melatonin_factor(&self) -> f32 {
2076        let phase = self.circadian_phase;
2077        // Hermite smoothstep for sharp but continuous transitions.
2078        let smoothstep = |t: f32| -> f32 {
2079            if t <= 0.0 {
2080                0.0
2081            } else if t >= 1.0 {
2082                1.0
2083            } else {
2084                t * t * (3.0 - 2.0 * t)
2085            }
2086        };
2087
2088        if (0.70..0.85).contains(&phase) {
2089            // Evening onset: rise over ~3.6 hours
2090            smoothstep((phase - 0.70) / 0.15)
2091        } else if !(0.15..0.85).contains(&phase) {
2092            // Biological night: plateau
2093            1.0
2094        } else if (0.15..0.30).contains(&phase) {
2095            // Morning offset: fall over ~3.6 hours
2096            1.0 - smoothstep((phase - 0.15) / 0.15)
2097        } else {
2098            // Day: suppressed
2099            0.0
2100        }
2101    }

2103    /// Basal HPA-axis drive from the circadian phase, range [0.2, 1.0].
2104    ///
2105    /// Cortisol is not a stress-only signal: the SCN drives tonic HPA
2106    /// secretion with a marked circadian profile (SCN → PVN → adrenal;
2107    /// Buijs et al., 1993/1999). The rhythm is endogenous — it persists
2108    /// under constant conditions and is only modestly suppressed by
2109    /// sleep itself — so it is read from `circadian_phase`, not from
2110    /// the sleep state (Weitzman et al., 1971; Scheer et al., 2009;
2111    /// Wehr et al., 2001).
2112    ///
2113    /// Profile (phase convention matches `melatonin_factor`:
2114    /// 0.0 = midnight, day spans 0.30–0.70):
2115    /// - 0.00–0.125 (00:00–03:00): nadir — low but nonzero. Basal
2116    ///   glucocorticoid tone is required for normal function and for
2117    ///   receptor resensitization; a zero floor is pathology, not rest.
2118    /// - 0.125–0.375 (03:00–09:00): steep anticipatory rise, peaking
2119    ///   near habitual wake — the morning surge region.
2120    /// - 0.375–0.60 (09:00–14:24): decline toward the quiescent tail.
2121    /// - 0.60–1.00 (14:24–24:00): slow tail back to the nadir.
2122    ///
2123    /// This factor scales [`HPA_BASAL_DRIVE`] into the ACTH target, so
2124    /// the basal level is an emergent production/clearance equilibrium
2125    /// through the real cascade — not a baseline override. (An earlier
2126    /// attempt modulated the cortisol *baseline* directly, which
2127    /// fought the cascade's clearance and was removed; secretion-side
2128    /// drive preserves the cascade.)
2129    pub fn hpa_circadian_factor(&self) -> f32 {
2130        let phase = self.circadian_phase;
2131        // Hermite smoothstep for smooth but continuous transitions.
2132        let smoothstep = |t: f32| -> f32 {
2133            let t = crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(t, 0.0);
2134            t * t * (3.0 - 2.0 * t)
2135        };
2136        const NADIR: f32 = 0.2;
2137        const TAIL: f32 = 0.35;
2138        if phase < 0.125 {
2139            NADIR
2140        } else if phase < 0.375 {
2141            // Pre-waking surge: 03:00–09:00
2142            NADIR + (1.0 - NADIR) * smoothstep((phase - 0.125) / 0.25)
2143        } else if phase < 0.60 {
2144            // Morning decline: 09:00–14:24
2145            1.0 - (1.0 - TAIL) * smoothstep((phase - 0.375) / 0.225)
2146        } else {
2147            // Quiescent tail: 14:24–24:00
2148            TAIL - (TAIL - NADIR) * smoothstep((phase - 0.60) / 0.40)
2149        }
2150    }

2152    /// Circadian baseline modifier for a given chemical.
2153    ///
2154    /// Returns a multiplicative factor that modulates the homeostatic
2155    /// baseline based on the circadian phase. This captures diurnal
2156    /// rhythms in neurotransmitter and hormone levels:
2157    ///
2158    /// - **Cortisol**: NO baseline modulation — the cortisol circadian
2159    ///   rhythm is real (SCN-driven, persists without a sleep cycle),
2160    ///   but it belongs on the *secretion* side: `hpa_circadian_factor`
2161    ///   scales a basal ACTH drive in the HPA step so the rhythm emerges
2162    ///   from the production/clearance equilibrium. Modulating the
2163    ///   baseline here created a constant floor that fought the
2164    ///   cascade's clearance and was removed. Returns 1.0.
2165    /// - **Dopamine**: daytime elevation. Slightly higher during the day,
2166    ///   peaking around noon.
2167    ///   Modulation: `1.0 + 0.15 * sin(2π * (phase - 0.25))`
2168    /// - **Serotonin**: precursor to melatonin. Reduced at night when
2169    ///   melatonin production is high (biochemical conversion).
2170    ///   Modulation: `1.0 - 0.10 * melatonin_factor`
2171    /// - **Histamine**: daytime elevation (promotes wakefulness),
2172    ///   peaking around noon.
2173    ///   Modulation: `1.0 + 0.20 * sin(2π * (phase - 0.25))`
2174    /// - **Melatonin**: driven by the circadian oscillator directly
2175    ///   (handled by melatonin secretion, not baseline modulation).
2176    ///   Returns 1.0 here — the oscillator handles it.
2177    /// - All other chemicals: 1.0 (no circadian modulation).
2178    pub fn circadian_baseline_modifier(&self, id: NeurochemicalId) -> f32 {
2179        let phase = self.circadian_phase;
2180        let two_pi = 2.0 * core::f32::consts::PI;
2181        match id {
2182            // Cortisol: NO baseline modulation. The circadian rhythm
2183            // is driven on the secretion side — `hpa_circadian_factor`
2184            // scales the basal ACTH drive, so cortisol's daily profile
2185            // emerges from the production/clearance equilibrium. A
2186            // baseline modifier here created a constant floor that
2187            // suppressed BDNF and blocked learning.
2188            NeurochemicalId::Dopamine => {
2189                // Daytime elevation: sin peaks at noon (phase 0.5)
2190                1.0 + 0.15 * (two_pi * (phase - 0.25)).sin()
2191            }
2192            NeurochemicalId::Serotonin => {
2193                // Serotonin is converted to melatonin at night
2194                let mel_fac = self.melatonin_factor();
2195                1.0 - 0.10 * mel_fac
2196            }
2197            NeurochemicalId::Histamine => {
2198                // Daytime elevation: promotes wakefulness
2199                // Melatonin attenuates the circadian drive to the
2200                // wake-promoting histamine system, which is the
2201                // documented mechanism: melatonin "acts at the SCN to
2202                // attenuate the wake-promoting signal of the circadian
2203                // clock, thus promoting sleep" (Liu et al. 1997), and
2204                // its sleep effect is "mostly ascribed to the circadian
2205                // component of sleep regulation" rather than to
2206                // relieving homeostatic pressure (Arbon et al. 2015 via
2207                // Zisapel 2018). Melatonin previously instead lowered
2208                // the homeostatic adenosine threshold, which both
2209                // misassigned the mechanism and let a circadian signal
2210                // drive Process S.
2211                let mel = self.chemicals[NeurochemicalId::Melatonin as usize].level;
2212                1.0 + 0.20 * (two_pi * (phase - 0.25)).sin() * (1.0 - 0.7 * mel)
2213            }
2214            // Melatonin is driven by the oscillator, not baseline modulation
2215            _ => 1.0,
2216        }
2217    }

2221    /// Recompute all derived fields: effective levels, summary metrics,
2222    /// plasticity gate, and emergent phase.
2223    ///
2224    /// This must be called after any change to chemical levels or
2225    /// receptor sensitivities, and after every dynamics tick.
2226    fn recompute_derived_with_dt(&mut self, dt_scale: f32, network: Option<NetworkLevels>) {
2227        // Effective levels — for GABA, apply allosteric modulation.
2228        // For DA and SRT, add a phasic boost: phasic bursts activate
2229        // low-affinity receptors (D1 for DA, 5-HT2A for SRT) that
2230        // increase effective signaling beyond the tonic baseline.
2231        for (i, chem) in self.chemicals.iter().enumerate() {
2232            let id = NeurochemicalId::from_u8(chem.id);
2233            let base = match id {
2234                NeurochemicalId::GABA => {
2235                    let b = chem.subtype_effective_level();
2236                    crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(b * (1.0 + self.gaba_a_allosteric * 0.5), b)
2237                }
2238                _ => chem.subtype_effective_level(),
2239            };
2240            // Phasic boost: for DA, phasic activates D1 (excitatory);
2241            // for SRT, phasic activates 5-HT2A (excitatory).
2242            // The boost is proportional to phasic_level × subtype weight.
2243            self.effective_levels[i] = match id {
2244                NeurochemicalId::Dopamine => {
2245                    let d1_weight = chem.receptor_subtypes[0];
2246                    crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(
2247                        base + chem.phasic_level * d1_weight * 0.3,
2248                        base,
2249                    )
2250                }
2251                NeurochemicalId::Serotonin => {
2252                    let ht2a_weight = chem.receptor_subtypes[1];
2253                    crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(
2254                        base + chem.phasic_level * ht2a_weight * 0.2,
2255                        base,
2256                    )
2257                }
2258                _ => base,
2259            };
2260        }
2261
2262        let eff = |id: NeurochemicalId| -> f32 { self.effective_levels[id as usize] };
2263
2264        // Arousal equation with orexin, epinephrine, and bistable flip-flop.
2265        //
2266        // The brain's sleep-wake system is a bistable flip-flop:
2267        // - Arousal promoters (orexin, histamine, NE, DA, ACh, EPI) push UP
2268        // - Sleep promoters (GABA, adenosine) push DOWN
2269        // - Orexin is the key stabilizer of the flip-flop (mutual excitation
2270        //   with histamine and NE, mutual inhibition with sleep promoters)
2271        //
2272        // The bistable property means the system tends to be either fully
2273        // awake or fully asleep, with rapid transitions between states.
2274        // We model this with hysteresis: when already awake, it takes
2275        // stronger sleep pressure to transition to sleep, and vice versa.
2276        //
2277        // Weights (arousal promoters):
2278        // - NE: 0.20 (vigilance)
2279        // - Histamine: 0.15 (wake maintenance)
2280        // - DA: 0.15 (reward-driven alertness)
2281        // - ACh: 0.15 (cortical activation)
2282        // - Orexin: 0.20 (major arousal stabilizer)
2283        // - Epinephrine: 0.10 (fast stress arousal)
2284        // Weights (sleep promoters):
2285        // - GABA: 0.25 (primary inhibitory — toned down from 0.30
2286        //   because GABA's baseline (0.50) is high even during
2287        //   wakefulness; at 0.30 the sleep promoters (0.315) exceeded
2288        //   the arousal promoters (0.285) at baseline, making the
2289        //   Wilson-Cowan equilibrium dip to ~0.26 at night — barely
2290        //   above the delta threshold (0.25), leaving Genesis stuck in
2291        //   delta after waking. At 0.25 the baseline equilibrium is
2292        //   ~0.33 (theta), and sleep is still maintained because
2293        //   adenosine (weight 0.65) dominates the sleep drive.)
2294        // - Adenosine: 0.65 (sleep pressure — weighted more than GABA
2295        //   because adenosine is the primary homeostatic sleep drive)
2296        let ne = eff(NeurochemicalId::Norepinephrine);
2297        let hist = eff(NeurochemicalId::Histamine);
2298        let da = eff(NeurochemicalId::Dopamine);
2299        let ach = eff(NeurochemicalId::Acetylcholine);
2300        let gaba = eff(NeurochemicalId::GABA);
2301        let adn = eff(NeurochemicalId::Adenosine);
2302        let orexin = eff(NeurochemicalId::Orexin);
2303
2304        // Circadian wake drive (Process C in the two-process model of
2305        // sleep, Borbély 1982). The SCN provides a wake-promoting signal
2306        // that counteracts homeostatic sleep pressure (adenosine) during
2307        // the day. Without this, the system has Process S (adenosine
2308        // accumulation) but no Process C, so it cannot maintain
2309        // wakefulness during the day even with healthy neurochemicals.
2310        //
2311        // The drive follows a sinusoidal rhythm that peaks at midday
2312        // (phase 0.5) and is near zero during the biological night.
2313        // It is scaled by 0.30 so that at peak it contributes +0.30 to
2314        // net_drive — enough to counteract moderate adenosine levels
2315        // during the day while still allowing high sleep pressure or
2316        // nighttime melatonin to overcome it.
2317        let circadian_wake = 0.30
2318            * (1.0 + (core::f32::consts::PI * 2.0 * (self.circadian_phase - 0.25)).sin())
2319            / 2.0;
2320        let epi = eff(NeurochemicalId::Epinephrine);
2321
2322        let arousal_promoters =
2323            ne * 0.20 + hist * 0.15 + da * 0.15 + ach * 0.15 + orexin * 0.20 + epi * 0.10;
2324        // Melatonin is a hormonal sleep-promoter (MT1/MT2 receptor
2325        // activation) and supplements the direct GABA/adenosine sleep
2326        // drive, especially at the circadian night.
2327        let mel = eff(NeurochemicalId::Melatonin);
2328        let sleep_promoters = gaba * 0.25 + adn * 0.65 + mel * 0.35;
2329
2330        // Graded arousal readout driven by neurochemical balance.
2331        //
2332        // The brain's sleep-wake system is a bistable flip-flop with two
2333        // mutually inhibiting populations: wake-active neurons (orexin,
2334        // histamine, NE) and sleep-active neurons (VLPO GABA, adenosine).
2335        // This equation is the *readout* of that competition, not the
2336        // switch itself:
2337        //
2338        //   a(t+1) = sigmoid(gain * (net_drive + se(a)))
2339        //   net_drive = promoters - sleep_promoters + circadian_wake
2340        //   se(a) = WC_SELF_EXCITATION * tanh(SLOPE * (a - 0.5))        //
2341        // It was originally written as an unbounded Wilson-Cowan
2342        // oscillator with a large linear self-excitation, on the theory
2343        // that the resulting bistability *was* the sleep-wake switch.
2344        // Solving the fixed-point equation showed that was not what it
2345        // did: the loop gain cleared the bistability threshold by only
2346        // 2.5%, which made the readout a knife-edge switch between
2347        // ~0.10 and ~0.90 with no moderate wake state in between. Since
2348        // resting `net_drive` is slightly positive (the circadian wake
2349        // drive contributes up to +0.30), Genesis locked near 0.9 while
2350        // idle and never came down. See `WC_SELF_EXCITATION`.
2351        //
2352        // Bounding the self-excitation with `tanh` keeps a mild
2353        // self-centering tendency — deviations from mid-arousal are
2354        // damped rather than amplified — while leaving the response
2355        // monotone and graded in chemistry. Sleep-wake switching is
2356        // handled by the `MentalPhase` thresholds and by the
2357        // network-driven arousal target below, which follows the
2358        // sleep-wake network's populations once a sleep stage is
2359        // entered and so provides far stronger hysteresis than a soft
2360        // feedback term ever did.
2361        //
2362        // (Wilson & Cowan, 1972; Saper, Chou & Scammell, 2005)
2363        let current_arousal = self.arousal;
2364        let net_drive = arousal_promoters - sleep_promoters + circadian_wake;
2365        let self_excitation =
2366            WC_SELF_EXCITATION * ((current_arousal - 0.5) * WC_SELF_EXCITATION_SLOPE).tanh();
2367        let total_input = (net_drive + self_excitation) * AROUSAL_GAIN;
2368        // Sigmoid with overflow protection: for |x| > 20, exp(-x) is
2369        // either ~0 or ~5e9, so we clamp to avoid NaN from overflow.
2370        let sigmoid = |x: f32| -> f32 {
2371            if x > 20.0 {
2372                1.0
2373            } else if x < -20.0 {
2374                0.0
2375            } else {
2376                1.0 / (1.0 + (-x).exp())
2377            }
2378        };
2379        // With the sleep-wake network present, cortical arousal is set by its
2380        // populations: LC for waking activation, REM-on for the REM activation
2381        // that the desynchronized EEG shows. The coefficients put NREM, REM and
2382        // waking at about 0.25, 0.48 and 0.78 on the measured stage means —
2383        // NREM still deep below the N1 descent gate (0.35), LC at the
2384        // switch threshold still above the drowsy gate (0.52). Those
2385        // bands were never sourced, so the REM level is open: it should
2386        // be checked against an EEG reference.
2387        // Without the network, the Wilson-Cowan equation is used, unchanged.
2388        let arousal_target = match network {
2389            Some(net) => {
2390                // Align arousal with sleep-wake network threshold:
2391                // LC at SLEEP_THRESHOLD (4.0 Hz / 6.0 Hz = 0.667) should
2392                // produce arousal above DROWSY_AROUSAL_GATE (0.52).
2393                // Formula: 0.15 + 0.65 * lc + 0.25 * rem
2394                // At lc=0.667: arousal = 0.15 + 0.65*0.667 = 0.58 > 0.52 ✓
2395                crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(0.15 + 0.65 * net.lc + 0.25 * net.rem, 0.0)
2396            }
2397            None => crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(sigmoid(total_input), 0.0),
2398        };
2399        // Exponential approach toward the target for time-invariance.
2400        // The previous `dt_scale.min(1.0)` factor was not
2401        // time-invariant: at 5 Hz (dt_scale=2) arousal fully replaced
2402        // the target every tick (no inertia), while at 20 Hz
2403        // (dt_scale=0.5) it only moved halfway. The exponential form
2404        // `1 - exp(-lambda * dt)` gives the same step response
2405        // regardless of tick rate. `lambda = 10` matches the original
2406        // 10 Hz behavior where `dt_scale = 1.0` → factor ≈ 0.632.
2407        // `dt = dt_scale * DT` (seconds per tick).
2408        let dt_seconds = dt_scale * DT;
2409        let arousal_alpha = 1.0 - (-10.0_f32 * dt_seconds).exp();
2410        self.arousal += (arousal_target - self.arousal) * arousal_alpha;
2411        self.arousal = crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(self.arousal, 0.0);
2412
2413        // Arousal is not clamped to a sleep-stage range. The sleep-wake
2414        // network drives the chemistry that sets arousal (LC and REM-on set
2415        // norepinephrine and acetylcholine; VLPO sets GABA), so arousal in
2416        // NREM and REM comes out of those levels, not from fixed caps.
2417        // Valence equation — neuroscientifically grounded.
2418        //
2419        // Positive contributors:
2420        // - DA (reward, wanting): 0.25
2421        // - Serotonin (mood stability): 0.25
2422        // - Oxytocin (social bonding, trust): 0.10
2423        // - Endorphin (euphoria, pleasure): 0.15
2424        // - NE (noradrenergic arousal affects mood): 0.10
2425        //
2426        // Negative contributors:
2427        // - Cortisol (stress, distress): -0.35
2428        // - Glutamate excess (too much → agitation): -0.15
2429        //   (only applies when glutamate > 0.70, modeling excitotoxicity-
2430        //   adjacent agitation from excess excitatory drive)
2431        //
2432        // Nonlinear interaction:
2433        // - DA × SRT synergy: 0.10 × DA × SRT
2434        //   (dopamine and serotonin synergize for positive mood —
2435        //   when both are high, valence is more positive than the
2436        //   linear sum would predict, modeling the "balanced mood"
2437        //   state where reward and mood stability co-occur)
2438        let da = eff(NeurochemicalId::Dopamine);
2439        let srt = eff(NeurochemicalId::Serotonin);
2440        let oxy = eff(NeurochemicalId::Oxytocin);
2441        let end = eff(NeurochemicalId::Endorphin);
2442        let cort = eff(NeurochemicalId::Cortisol);
2443        let glu = eff(NeurochemicalId::Glutamate);
2444        let ne = eff(NeurochemicalId::Norepinephrine);
2445
2446        let glu_excess = (glu - 0.70).max(0.0);
2447        let da_srt_synergy = da * srt * 0.10;
2448        // Cortisol's valence drag and plasticity suppression are written
2449        // for a hormone on its full [0, 1] range; the substrate's
2450        // reachable envelope is ~0.02–0.08, so the effects are scaled by
2451        // the fraction of that envelope in use (see CORTISOL_PEAK_LEVEL).
2452        let cort_stress = cortisol_stress_fraction(cort);
2453
2454        self.valence = da * 0.25 + srt * 0.25 + oxy * 0.10 + end * 0.15 + ne * 0.10
2455            - cort_stress * 0.35
2456            - glu_excess * 0.15
2457            + da_srt_synergy;
2458        self.valence = crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(self.valence, 0.0);
2459
2460        // Global tone: mean of effective levels
2461        let sum: f32 = self.effective_levels.iter().sum();
2462        self.global_tone = crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(sum / NEUROCHEMICAL_COUNT as f32, 0.0);
2463
2464        // Plasticity gate: BDNF amplitude × the corticosteroid LTP
2465        // inverted-U (Diamond et al., 1992; MR/GR ratio model, de Kloet
2466        // et al., 1999) — optimal at intermediate cortisol, degraded
2467        // toward both adrenalectomy-low and GR-saturated levels.
2468        let bdnf = eff(NeurochemicalId::BDNF);
2469        self.plasticity_gate =
2470            crate::state::sanitize::finite_or(bdnf * corticosteroid_ltp_facilitation(cort), 0.0);
2471
2472        // Emergent phase
2473        self.emergent_phase = self.compute_phase() as u8;
2474    }

2476    /// One-time wake recovery boost for daemon restart.
2477    ///
2478    /// When the daemon restarts with a persisted state from a previous
2479    /// session, neurochemicals may be depleted (BDNF, dopamine, serotonin)
2480    /// from prior stress or activity. The homeostatic recovery is slow
2481    /// (second-order dynamics), so the system can stay in a "guarded"
2482    /// emotional state for minutes after restart even though there is
2483    /// no ongoing stress.
2484    ///
2485    /// This method gives depleted neurotransmitters (not stress hormones)
2486    /// a one-time boost toward baseline when cortisol is low (no ongoing
2487    /// stress). It is biologically grounded: after sleep, the brain
2488    /// rapidly restores depleted neurochemicals. A daemon restart is
2489    /// analogous to waking up.
2490    ///
2491    /// Stress hormones (cortisol, CRH) are NOT boosted — they have no
2492    /// homeostatic set-point and should stay at zero when there is no
2493    /// stressor.
2494    ///
2495    /// `network` is the sleep-wake populations' current levels — the
2496    /// recompute after the boost must read arousal off the body's own
2497    /// switch, not the chemistry-only fallback.
2498    pub fn wake_recovery(&mut self, network: NetworkLevels) {
2499        let cort = self.chemicals[NeurochemicalId::Cortisol as usize].level;
2500        // Only recover if there is no ongoing stress. The gate is the
2501        // stress-phase boundary — cortisol's reachable envelope tops
2502        // out around ~0.08, so the previous 0.3 could never block.
2503        if cort > STRESS_CORTISOL_GATE {
2504            return;
2505        }
2506        // Boost depleted neurotransmitters toward baseline and
2507        // resensitize downregulated receptors. A daemon restart is
2508        // analogous to waking up — after sleep, the brain rapidly
2509        // restores depleted neurochemicals and resensitizes receptors
2510        // that were downregulated by sustained high levels in the
2511        // previous session.
2512        for (i, chem_id) in NeurochemicalId::all().into_iter().enumerate() {
2513            // Skip stress hormones — they have no baseline and should
2514            // stay at zero when there is no stressor.
2515            if matches!(chem_id, NeurochemicalId::Cortisol | NeurochemicalId::CRH) {
2516                continue;
2517            }
2518            let baseline = self.chemicals[i].baseline;
2519            let level = self.chemicals[i].level;
2520            let genetic = chem_id.default_baseline();
2521            // Boost depleted levels toward baseline.
2522            if level < baseline {
2523                let gap = baseline - level;
2524                self.chemicals[i].level = level + gap * 0.5;
2525                self.chemicals[i].velocity = 0.0;
2526            } else if level < genetic * 0.9 {
2527                // Baseline has drifted down with the level. Boost
2528                // toward the genetic baseline instead.
2529                let gap = genetic - level;
2530                self.chemicals[i].level = level + gap * 0.5;
2531                self.chemicals[i].baseline = genetic;
2532                self.chemicals[i].velocity = 0.0;
2533            }
2534            // Resensitize receptors toward their default state. A
2535            // daemon restart is analogous to waking up — after sleep,
2536            // the brain rapidly restores receptor sensitivity and
2537            // reverses desensitization/internalization that built up
2538            // from sustained high levels in the previous session.
2539            // Move each adaptation factor 50% of the way back to 1.0.
2540            let sensitivity = self.chemicals[i].receptor_sensitivity;
2541            if sensitivity < 0.9 {
2542                self.chemicals[i].receptor_sensitivity = sensitivity + (1.0 - sensitivity) * 0.5;
2543            }
2544            let desens = self.chemicals[i].desensitization_factor;
2545            if desens < 0.9 {
2546                self.chemicals[i].desensitization_factor = desens + (1.0 - desens) * 0.5;
2547            }
2548            let intern = self.chemicals[i].internalization_factor;
2549            if intern < 0.9 {
2550                self.chemicals[i].internalization_factor = intern + (1.0 - intern) * 0.5;
2551            }
2552        }
2553        // Recompute derived fields (effective levels, arousal, valence,
2554        // plasticity_gate, phase) from the boosted levels and
2555        // resensitized receptors, on the body's own arousal readout.
2556        self.recompute_derived_with_network(network);
2557    }

2575    /// Compute the emergent mental phase from the current neurochemical
2576    /// state. This is not a manual switch — it's a derived property of
2577    /// the coupled dynamics.
2578    ///
2579    /// Uses hysteresis to prevent phase flickering: the threshold for
2580    /// *entering* a phase is stricter than the threshold for *staying*
2581    /// in it. This means the system won't rapidly oscillate between
2582    /// phases when neurochemical levels are near a boundary. The
2583    /// hysteresis margin is 0.05–0.10 for each threshold, matching
2584    /// the smoothness of real phase transitions in dynamical systems.
2585    fn compute_phase(&self) -> MentalPhase {
2586        let current = MentalPhase::from_u8(self.emergent_phase);
2587        // NREM and REM are owned by the sleep-wake network — this
2588        // classifier names only the waking phases, so it can neither
2589        // produce nor legitimately clear a sleep stage. Recomputing
2590        // derived state used to overwrite a network-stamped NREM/REM
2591        // with a waking phase on every recompute_derived call (the
2592        // inference-feedback pass in advance_neuro, the impulse and
2593        // baseline IPC handlers, wake_recovery), so the wire never
2594        // showed the body as asleep — the mind ran conversation while
2595        // the network sat in NREM and adenosine stayed pinned at its
2596        // clearance floor. Only apply_network_phase may enter or exit
2597        // a sleep stage.
2598        if matches!(current, MentalPhase::NREM | MentalPhase::REM) {
2599            return current;
2600        }
2601        self.compute_phase_with_hysteresis(current)
2602    }

2604    /// Compute phase with hysteresis — the current phase gets easier
2605    /// thresholds to stay in, while new phases require stricter thresholds.
2606    fn compute_phase_with_hysteresis(&self, current: MentalPhase) -> MentalPhase {
2607        let eff = |id: NeurochemicalId| -> f32 { self.effective_levels[id as usize] };
2608        let raw = |id: NeurochemicalId| -> f32 { self.chemicals[id as usize].level };
2609
2610        let adn_eff = eff(NeurochemicalId::Adenosine);
2611        let adn_raw = raw(NeurochemicalId::Adenosine);
2612        let hist = eff(NeurochemicalId::Histamine);
2613        let cort = eff(NeurochemicalId::Cortisol);
2614        let ne = eff(NeurochemicalId::Norepinephrine);
2615        let srt = eff(NeurochemicalId::Serotonin);
2616        let da = eff(NeurochemicalId::Dopamine);
2617        let ach = eff(NeurochemicalId::Acetylcholine);
2618        let gaba = eff(NeurochemicalId::GABA);
2619        let mel = eff(NeurochemicalId::Melatonin);
2620        let ox = eff(NeurochemicalId::Orexin);
2621
2622        // Adenosine sleep signal: blend of effective (receptor-mediated)
2623        // and raw (metabolic) levels. Adenosine's somnogenic effect is
2624        // partly receptor-independent — it directly inhibits excitatory
2625        // neurons via volume transmission and extrasynaptic A1 receptors
2626        // that are not subject to the same downregulation as synaptic
2627        // receptors (Porkka-Heiskanen & Kalinchuk, 2011). Using a 50/50
2628        // blend ensures that high metabolic pressure still gates
2629        // Drowsy — and feeds the mind's sleep-pressure signal — even
2630        // when receptor sensitivity is at floor (0.50). Sleep onset
2631        // itself is owned by the sleep-wake network, not by this
2632        // classifier.
2633        let adn = adn_eff * 0.5 + adn_raw * 0.5;
2634
2635        // Hysteresis margins: easier to stay than to enter
2636        // Enter threshold requires stricter conditions; stay threshold relaxes by margin
2637        let h = 0.08; // hysteresis margin
2638
2639        // The sleep stages (NREM, REM) are not classified here: the
2640        // sleep-wake network owns them and injects them via
2641        // `apply_network_phase`. This classifier names only the waking
2642        // phases.
2643        //
2644        // Overwhelmed: cortisol and NE both at their ceiling.
2645        //
2646        // The previous gate also required `global_tone > 0.7`, which
2647        // is unreachable by construction: `global_tone` is the mean of
2648        // all 18 effective levels, most of which sit at fixed genetic
2649        // baselines, so it measures ~0.25 in every regime. Measured
2650        // peaks over two hours under drive:
2651        //
2652        //   rest 0.263   stress 0.255   vigilance 0.248   debt 0.250
2653        //
2654        // It does not discriminate stress at all — cortisol moving from
2655        // 0.020 to 0.084 shifts the mean of 18 chemicals by 0.004. So
2656        // the conjunction could never be satisfied and Overwhelmed was
2657        // unreachable no matter what the body did.
2658        //
2659        // Overwhelm is now defined by the two mediators that actually
2660        // move, both set above the Stress gates so the ordering
2661        // Stress < Overwhelmed is preserved.
2662        // Scaled from the stress gates so the ordering
2663        // Stress < Overwhelmed is preserved, but kept inside the
2664        // measured envelope. At 1.4x/1.3x the gates were 0.084 and
2665        // 0.585 against measured maxima of 0.084 and 0.578 — at or
2666        // above the ceiling, so Overwhelm could not be entered. The
2667        // multipliers are now 1.15x and 1.13x, which sit just above the
2668        // stress gates (0.069, 0.509) and below the maxima.
2669        let ow_cort = if current == MentalPhase::Overwhelmed {
2670            STRESS_CORTISOL_GATE * 1.1
2671        } else {
2672            STRESS_CORTISOL_GATE * 1.15
2673        };
2674        let ow_ne = if current == MentalPhase::Overwhelmed {
2675            STRESS_NOREPI_GATE * 1.07
2676        } else {
2677            STRESS_NOREPI_GATE * 1.13
2678        };
2679        if cort > ow_cort && ne > ow_ne {
2680            return MentalPhase::Overwhelmed;
2681        }
2682
2683        // Stress response: cortisol high, NE high, low serotonin.
2684        //
2685        // The gates are expressed against the substrate's *measured*
2686        // reachable envelope rather than absolute numbers, because the
2687        // absolute numbers were written for a cortisol scale this
2688        // substrate does not produce. Measured peaks over two hours:
2689        //
2690        //   cortisol  resting 0.020, under sustained drive 0.084
2691        //   norepi    resting 0.306, under sustained drive 0.578
2692        //
2693        // The old gates of 0.65 and 0.60 were therefore unreachable by
2694        // 7.7x and 1.04x respectively — the stress phase could not be
2695        // entered under any drive. Note the cortisol ceiling is
2696        // structural, not a tuning gap: at equilibrium
2697        // `level ≈ acth * gain / clearance`, and ACTH saturates at
2698        // 0.151, giving 0.049; the measured 0.084 is the transient
2699        // peak. Raising the ACTH-to-cortisol gain 344x to reach 0.65
2700        // would not be physiologically defensible, so the gate moves
2701        // to meet the real range instead.
2702        //
2703        // Placed at ~70% of the measured cortisol maximum and ~78% of
2704        // the NE maximum: high enough that ordinary physiological
2705        // variation cannot trigger a stress state (resting cortisol is
2706        // 3x below the gate), low enough that a genuine drive reaches
2707        // it.
2708        let stress_cort = if current == MentalPhase::Stress {
2709            STRESS_CORTISOL_GATE * 0.88
2710        } else {
2711            STRESS_CORTISOL_GATE
2712        };
2713        let stress_ne = if current == MentalPhase::Stress {
2714            STRESS_NOREPI_GATE * 0.9
2715        } else {
2716            STRESS_NOREPI_GATE
2717        };
2718        if cort > stress_cort && ne > stress_ne && srt < 0.35 + h {
2719            return MentalPhase::Stress;
2720        }
2721
2722        // Flow: high dopamine, high ACh, moderate NE, low cortisol.
2723        //
2724        // The dopamine gate was 0.65 against a measured maximum of
2725        // 0.42 across every drive regime, so Flow was unreachable for
2726        // the same reason Stress was: a threshold written for a scale
2727        // this substrate does not produce. Resting dopamine is 0.384
2728        // and it peaks at 0.416, so 0.55/0.45 is 1.4x resting with
2729        // headroom for a genuine engagement state.
2730        //
2731        // The cortisol exclusion had the same scale problem: 0.30 sat
2732        // above the entire reachable envelope (~0.08 peak), so flow
2733        // could fire during active stress. The gate is now the
2734        // stress-phase boundary itself — flow requires cortisol out of
2735        // stress territory, matching the biology (glucocorticoid tone
2736        // dissolves flow). No `+h` margin: the hysteresis term is
2737        // wider than the whole cortisol range and would push the
2738        // bound above the peak again.
2739        let flow_da = if current == MentalPhase::Flow {
2740            FLOW_DOPAMINE_GATE * 0.88
2741        } else {
2742            FLOW_DOPAMINE_GATE
2743        };
2744        let flow_ach = if current == MentalPhase::Flow {
2745            FLOW_ACETYLCHOLINE_GATE * 0.88
2746        } else {
2747            FLOW_ACETYLCHOLINE_GATE
2748        };
2749        if da > flow_da && ach > flow_ach && ne > 0.40 && cort < STRESS_CORTISOL_GATE {
2750            return MentalPhase::Flow;
2751        }
2752
2753        // Alert: high NE + high histamine, low GABA, with orexin and
2754        // melatonin gating. Orexin is a wake stabilizer (lowers the NE/
2755        // histamine threshold); melatonin suppresses alertness. This makes
2756        // alertness a coordinated arousal decision rather than NE alone.
2757        let alert_ox_bonus = 0.10 * ox;
2758        // The histamine gate was 0.55 against a measured maximum of
2759        // 0.361, so Alert was unreachable regardless of NE. Histamine
2760        // rests at 0.320 and peaks at 0.361; a gate of 0.40 sits above
2761        // resting with margin but inside the reachable range.
2762        // Hysteresis: the gates drop once Alert is entered, so staying
2763        // alert is easier than entering it. The 0.94 factor is the
2764        // same style of margin used by the other phase gates.
2765        let alert_ne = if current == MentalPhase::Alert {
2766            ALERT_NOREPI_GATE * ALERT_STAY_FACTOR - alert_ox_bonus
2767        } else {
2768            ALERT_NOREPI_GATE - alert_ox_bonus
2769        };
2770        let alert_hist = if current == MentalPhase::Alert {
2771            ALERT_HISTAMINE_GATE * ALERT_STAY_FACTOR - alert_ox_bonus
2772        } else {
2773            ALERT_HISTAMINE_GATE - alert_ox_bonus
2774        };
2775        let alert_mel = if current == MentalPhase::Alert {
2776            0.40 + h
2777        } else {
2778            0.30
2779        };
2780        let alert_ox = if current == MentalPhase::Alert {
2781            0.15
2782        } else {
2783            0.20
2784        };
2785        if ne > alert_ne && hist > alert_hist && gaba < 0.40 + h && mel < alert_mel && ox > alert_ox
2786        {
2787            return MentalPhase::Alert;
2788        }
2789
2790        // Drowsy: awake, with sleep pressure building and arousal falling.
2791        //
2792        // Sleep itself is no longer decided here. The sleep-wake network
2793        // owns the boundary into sleep and the NREM/REM stages (see
2794        // `sleep_network::stage`), so this rule has no upper bound on
2795        // adenosine. Hysteresis on both axes keeps Drowsy from flickering.
2796        let drowsy_adn = if current == MentalPhase::Drowsy {
2797            DROWSY_ADENOSINE_GATE * DROWSY_STAY_FACTOR
2798        } else {
2799            DROWSY_ADENOSINE_GATE
2800        };
2801        let drowsy_arousal = if current == MentalPhase::Drowsy {
2802            DROWSY_AROUSAL_STAY
2803        } else {
2804            DROWSY_AROUSAL_GATE
2805        };
2806        // The adenosine conjunct names the usual descent — pressure
2807        // high, arousal falling through its gate. The N1 path names
2808        // the state the conjunct can't see: arousal already in the
2809        // deep-descent band on its own. The wake systems' readout
2810        // *is* the drowsiness at that depth — asking adenosine to
2811        // corroborate it is what let the phase claim Active while
2812        // the body's own axis said otherwise.
2813        let n1_arousal = if current == MentalPhase::Drowsy {
2814            N1_AROUSAL_STAY
2815        } else {
2816            N1_AROUSAL_GATE
2817        };
2818        if (adn > drowsy_adn && self.arousal < drowsy_arousal) || self.arousal < n1_arousal {
2819            return MentalPhase::Drowsy;
2820        }
2821
2822        // Drowsy ranks below every other waking phase: it is what waking looks
2823        // like when sleep pressure is high and arousal is low, and any stronger
2824        // waking state should be reported instead.
2825        MentalPhase::Active
2826    }

2835    /// Advance the neurochemical system by one tick using the given
2836    /// parameters.
2837    ///
2838    /// This is the core dynamics equation. For each chemical `i`:
2839    ///
2840    /// ```text
2841    /// homeostatic_force = (baseline[i] - level[i]) * homeostatic_rate
2842    /// coupling_force = Σ_j coupling_matrix[i][j] * (effective[j] - baseline[j])
2843    /// velocity[i] += (homeostatic_force + coupling_force) * dt
2844    /// velocity[i] *= damping
2845    /// level[i] += velocity[i] * dt
2846    /// ```
2847    ///
2848    /// The coupling force uses *effective* levels (level × sensitivity ×
2849    /// desensitization × internalization), not raw levels. This means a
2850    /// chemical with downregulated receptors exerts less influence on the
2851    /// system even if its concentration is high — which is exactly how
2852    /// tolerance works systemically.
2853    ///
2854    /// # New dynamics in v3
2855    ///
2856    /// - **Region-specific SRT→DA coupling**: the serotonin→dopamine
2857    ///   coupling depends on the active brain region (striatum vs PFC vs
2858    ///   default), modeling 5-HT2A (inhibitory) and 5-HT1A (facilitatory)
2859    ///   receptor subtype pathways.
2860    /// - **GABA disinhibition**: when GABA is very high (>0.8) AND ACh is
2861    ///   high (>0.6), GABA interneurons inhibit other GABA interneurons,
2862    ///   disinhibiting glutamate (a small positive coupling to GLU).
2863    /// - **BDNF recovery after stress**: when cortisol clears below the
2864    ///   stress-phase gate (`STRESS_CORTISOL_GATE`) and BDNF is below
2865    ///   baseline, BDNF gets a recovery boost proportional to serotonin
2866    ///   level (serotonin drives BDNF expression).
2867    /// - **Hard clamps**: cortisol is clamped to [0, cortisol_max] and NE
2868    ///   to [0, ne_max] to prevent runaway stress/arousal.
2869    /// - **Split receptor adaptation**: desensitization (fast,
2870    ///   phosphorylation-based) and internalization (slow, membrane
2871    ///   removal) are modeled as separate factors.
2872    /// - **Restoring force**: 20% of adaptation rate (increased from 10%
2873    ///   for faster recovery from chronic stress).
2874    ///
2875    /// # New dynamics in v3.1
2876    ///
2877    /// - **HPA axis cascade**: CRH → ACTH → cortisol with time delays
2878    ///   and negative feedback. CRH drives ACTH production (delayed),
2879    ///   ACTH drives cortisol release (delayed), cortisol feeds back
2880    ///   negatively to CRH. This replaces direct cortisol manipulation
2881    ///   with a biologically accurate cascade through the pituitary
2882    ///   intermediate (ACTH).
2883    /// - **Tonic/phasic neurotransmitter distinction**: tonic_level
2884    ///   (slow background) and phasic_level (fast burst) are tracked
2885    ///   separately. Phasic decays quickly (seconds), tonic changes
2886    ///   slowly. For DA, tonic activates D2 (high-affinity) and phasic
2887    ///   activates D1 (low-affinity). For SRT, tonic activates 5-HT1A
2888    ///   and phasic activates 5-HT2A.
2889    /// - **Vesicular pool depletion**: impulses deplete the vesicular
2890    ///   pool, reducing the effect of subsequent impulses (synaptic
2891    ///   depression). Synthesis and reuptake slowly replenish the pool.
2892    /// - **Narcolepsy modeling**: when orexin is very low (<0.10),
2893    ///   histamine gets an extra downward force, modeling the sudden
2894    ///   sleep transitions (cataplexy) seen in narcolepsy.
2895    pub fn tick_with_params(&mut self, params: &NeuroTickParams) {
2896        // ─── Circuit breaker: sanitize all chemical fields ───────
2897        //
2898        // This is the last-resort defense against NaN/inf propagation.
2899        // If a non-finite value enters any chemical field between ticks
2900        // (via IPC, mmap race, corrupted model file, or a computation
2901        // bug we missed), the coupling matrix below would propagate
2902        // it to all 18 chemicals within a single tick, permanently
2903        // corrupting the mmap'd state.
2904        //
2905        // The circuit breaker scans every chemical's fields at the
2906        // START of the tick and resets any non-finite value to a safe
2907        // default. This confines the damage to one chemical (which
2908        // recovers via homeostatic forces) rather than letting it
2909        // poison the entire system.
2910        //
2911        // The defaults are chosen to be "neutral" — the chemical
2912        // recovers to its baseline via the normal homeostatic
2913        // mechanism over the next few ticks.
2914        for chem in &mut self.chemicals {
2915            let id = NeurochemicalId::from_u8(chem.id);
2916            let default_baseline = id.default_baseline();
2917            // Level: reset to baseline (the homeostatic set-point)
2918            if !chem.level.is_finite() {
2919                chem.level = default_baseline;
2920            }
2921            // Velocity: reset to zero (no momentum)
2922            if !chem.velocity.is_finite() {
2923                chem.velocity = 0.0;
2924            }
2925            // Baseline: reset to genetic default
2926            if !chem.baseline.is_finite() {
2927                chem.baseline = default_baseline;
2928            }
2929            // Tonic level: reset to baseline
2930            if !chem.tonic_level.is_finite() {
2931                chem.tonic_level = default_baseline;
2932            }
2933            // Phasic level: reset to zero (no burst)
2934            if !chem.phasic_level.is_finite() {
2935                chem.phasic_level = 0.0;
2936            }
2937            // Receptor sensitivity: reset to 1.0 (no adaptation)
2938            if !chem.receptor_sensitivity.is_finite() {
2939                chem.receptor_sensitivity = 1.0;
2940            }
2941            // Receptor subtypes: reset to defaults
2942            if !chem.receptor_subtypes[0].is_finite() || !chem.receptor_subtypes[1].is_finite() {
2943                chem.receptor_subtypes = id.default_receptor_subtypes();

3967        // cortisol. This creates a delayed, amplified stress response

4003            .max(0.0);

4011        // axis is never silent in a living body. Scaled by maturation
4012        // (SHRP blunts the whole axis) and the pituitary gain, exactly
4013        // like the CRH drive it sits beside.

4016            * pituitary_gain;

4027        // ACTH stimulates cortisol production in the adrenal cortex.
4028        // Scaled by maturation — the adrenal cortex is unresponsive
4029        // during the SHRP.

4031        // Applied directly to level (first-order). Convention in this

4033        // (dt_scale = dt/DT, time-invariant). 0.001/step at the 10 Hz
4034        // reference rate equals the documented 0.01/sec production
4035        // rate (0.01 * DT = 0.001), integrated once (not through

4039        // Runaway is prevented by the nonlinear CORTISOL_CLEARANCE_RATE

4069        // High cortisol inhibits hypothalamic CRH neurons, shutting
4070        // off the stressor signal. This is already present in the
4071        // coupling matrix as CORT→CRH = -0.10, which propagates
4072        // through the velocity system. The strong ACTH negative
4073        // feedback above is the primary long-loop brake; adding a

4080        //
4081        // Cortisol is cleared from the bloodstream by the liver. The

4084        // feedback that prevents cortisol from getting stuck at the
4085        // hard clamp.
4086        //
4087        // At rest (cortisol = baseline): clearance = 0, no effect.
4088        // Under stress (cortisol >> baseline): clearance is strong,

4123        // excitatory).

4208        // Receptor adaptation: split into three mechanisms ordered by
4209        // the biological cascade — the response to sustained agonist is
4210        //

4212        //     → internalization (minutes–hours)
4213        //       → density downregulation (hours–days)
4214        //
4215        // 1. Desensitization (fastest, phosphorylation-based, reversible
4216        //    in minutes): responds first to high levels. β2AR loses ~80%

4220        //    level > baseline and recovers when level ≤ baseline.

4222        // 2. Internalization (intermediate, membrane removal, reversible
4223        //    in tens of minutes to hours): endocytosis follows
4224        //    phosphorylation — ~55% of β2AR is internalized at 30 min.

4226        // 3. receptor_sensitivity (slowest, receptor density/expression,

4228        //    receptor number long after functional desensitization has
4229        //    run. Asymmetric rates: downregulation is ~3x faster than

4235        //    not gated on level-baseline deviation. This is the
4236        //    biological basis of sleep restoration: the brain uses
4237        //    the low-activity window to restore receptor function
4238        //    regardless of neurotransmitter levels. Without this,
4239        //    the system enters a "receptor burnout trap" where
4240        //    chronic stress elevates raw levels, receptors
4241        //    downregulate, effective levels collapse, but raw
4242        //    levels stay above the drifted baselines so the

4248            let chem_adapt_rate = adaptation_rate * chem_id.receptor_adaptation_multiplier();

4250            let deviation = chem.level - chem.baseline;

4252            // Receptor adaptation: each receptor factor adapts based on
4253            // the effective deviation (raw deviation scaled by receptor
4254            // health). This ensures that downregulation is proportional
4255            // to actual receptor stimulation, not raw ligand
4256            // concentration. When receptors are burnt out, effective
4257            // deviation is small, so downregulation is weak — allowing
4258            // receptor turnover to slowly restore function.
4259            //
4260            // Receptor turnover (RECEPTOR_TURNOVER_RATE) provides a
4261            // slow background recovery toward 1.0 that models
4262            // continuous receptor replacement. It's normally
4263            // overwhelmed by deviation-gated downregulation, but when
4264            // downregulation is weak (burnout), turnover slowly
4265            // restores receptor function. See the constant docs above
4266            // for the full rationale and calibration.
4267            // Effective deviation: scale the raw deviation by receptor
4268            // health. Receptor downregulation is driven by receptor
4269            // activation (effective signaling), not by raw ligand
4270            // concentration. When receptors are burnt out, effective
4271            // deviation is small, so downregulation is weak — allowing
4272            // receptor turnover to slowly restore function.
4273            //
4274            // Biologically, receptor downregulation responds to receptor
4275            // stimulation (effective signaling), not raw concentration.
4276            // When receptors are burnt out, effective stimulation is low,
4277            // so there's no biological reason to downregulate further.

4279                * chem.desensitization_factor
4280                * chem.internalization_factor;
4281            let effective_deviation = deviation * receptor_health;
4282
4283            // Receptor turnover: slow background recovery toward 1.0.
4284            // Models continuous receptor replacement (half-life days
4285            // to weeks). Normally overwhelmed by deviation-gated
4286            // downregulation, but when downregulation is weak (low
4287            // effective deviation due to burnout), turnover slowly
4288            // restores receptor function.
4289            //

4297            // as receptors burn out, and turnover then restores function:
4298            // - In normal overstimulation: desensitization carries the
4299            //   acute adaptation and recovers quickly once the drive
4300            //   drops — matching measured β2AR resensitization.
4301            // - In extreme burnout: effective deviation → 0, downregulation
4302            //   stalls, and turnover pulls every factor back toward 1.0.

4304            //   downregulated — chronic agonist produces chronic tolerance.

4306            let desens_turnover = RECEPTOR_TURNOVER_RATE * 5.0 * dt_scale;
4307            let intern_turnover = RECEPTOR_TURNOVER_RATE * dt_scale;

4311            // to days, long after functional desensitization has run.

4315                } else {
4316                    chem_adapt_rate * 0.06 // upregulation: ~3x slower still
4317                };

4319                // deviation for upregulation (upregulation should
4320                // respond to actual low levels, not effective levels).
4321                let adapt_deviation = if deviation > 0.0 {
4322                    effective_deviation
4323                } else {
4324                    deviation
4325                };
4326                chem.receptor_sensitivity -= adapt_deviation * rate * dt_scale;
4327            }
4328            // Sleep resensitization: unconditional recovery toward 1.0
4329            if sleep_resens > 0.0 {
4330                chem.receptor_sensitivity +=
4331                    (1.0 - chem.receptor_sensitivity) * sleep_resens * dt_scale;
4332            }
4333            // Receptor turnover (background recovery — synthesis is the
4334            // slowest replacement pathway)
4335            chem.receptor_sensitivity += (1.0 - chem.receptor_sensitivity) * sens_turnover;

4337            // the worst physiological case reported in the literature.
4338            // Key data points:
4339            // - D2/D3 (chronic stimulant abuse): 10-25% reduction
4340            //   (Volkow et al.; meta-analysis effect size -0.76,
4341            //    Bertoletti et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2017)
4342            // - β2AR (chronic albuterol, 2 weeks): 22-42% pulmonary
4343            //   reduction (Hayes et al., 1996)
4344            // - Oxytocin receptor (10-day continuous infusion): ≥50%
4345            //   in all target fields (Endocrinology 130:2602, 1992)
4346            // - 5-HT2A (chronic SSRI): 11-38% decrease
4347            // - MOR (chronic morphine): "small reduction in total
4348            //   receptor number" — the 70-80% loss is functional
4349            //   desensitization, not density (Chen et al., PNAS 1989)
4350            // The previous 0.1 floor allowed 90% reduction, which
4351            // combined with desensitization and internalization floors
4352            // produced a 1000× effective level reduction — biologically
4353            // absurd. 0.5 ensures graceful degradation: even at maximum
4354            // burnout, effective levels retain enough signal for basic
4355            // function and self-recovery.

4359            // Desensitization (fastest, minutes): responds first to current
4360            // deviation — phosphorylation/arrestin uncoupling is the
4361            // earliest adaptation mechanism (β2AR phosphorylation rises
4362            // 11–13× within 1 min of agonist exposure; January et al.,
4363            // JBC 1997). Use effective deviation for downregulation.
4364            let desens_rate = chem_adapt_rate * 5.0;

4367                    effective_deviation
4368                } else {
4369                    deviation
4370                };
4371                if deviation > 0.0 {
4372                    chem.desensitization_factor -= adapt_deviation * desens_rate * dt_scale;
4373                } else {

4375                    chem.desensitization_factor +=
4376                        (-adapt_deviation) * desens_rate * 0.5 * dt_scale;
4377                }
4378            }
4379            // Sleep resensitization: unconditional recovery toward 1.0
4380            if sleep_resens > 0.0 {
4381                chem.desensitization_factor +=
4382                    (1.0 - chem.desensitization_factor) * sleep_resens * dt_scale;
4383            }
4384            // Receptor turnover (background recovery — dephosphorylation/
4385            // resensitization is the fastest replacement pathway)
4386            chem.desensitization_factor += (1.0 - chem.desensitization_factor) * desens_turnover;
4387            // Floor at 0.2: maximum functional desensitization is ~80%,
4388            // per the literature on chronic agonist exposure:
4389            // - MOR (chronic morphine, 3+ days): 70-80% loss of
4390            //   receptor function via PKC-mediated uncoupling
4391            //   (Bailey et al., PMC2695152)
4392            // - β2AR (full agonist, epinephrine): ~70-80%
4393            //   desensitization of adenylyl cyclase response
4394            //   (January et al., JBC 1997)
4395            // - 5-HT1A (chronic SSRI): desensitization at G-protein
4396            //   coupling level, not receptor number (Blier et al.)
4397            // Note: this is functional uncoupling (phosphorylation/
4398            // arrestin), NOT receptor density loss — density is
4399            // handled by receptor_sensitivity above.
4400            chem.desensitization_factor =
4401                crate::state::sanitize::finite_clamp(chem.desensitization_factor, 0.2, 1.0);
4402
4403            // Internalization (intermediate, tens of minutes–hours):
4404            // endocytosis follows desensitization but precedes density
4405            // change — ~55% of β2AR is internalized within 30 min of
4406            // agonist exposure (January et al., JBC 1997; DAMGO/MOR
4407            // 50–58%). Use effective deviation for downregulation.
4408            let intern_rate = chem_adapt_rate;
4409            {
4410                let adapt_deviation = if deviation > 0.0 {
4411                    effective_deviation
4412                } else {
4413                    deviation
4414                };
4415                if deviation > 0.0 {
4416                    chem.internalization_factor -= adapt_deviation * intern_rate * dt_scale;
4417                } else {
4418                    // Very slow recovery toward 1.0
4419                    chem.internalization_factor +=
4420                        (-adapt_deviation) * intern_rate * 0.2 * dt_scale;
4421                }
4422            }
4423            // Sleep resensitization: unconditional recovery toward 1.0
4424            if sleep_resens > 0.0 {
4425                chem.internalization_factor +=
4426                    (1.0 - chem.internalization_factor) * sleep_resens * dt_scale;
4427            }
4428            // Receptor turnover (background recovery — membrane recycling
4429            // is intermediate)
4430            chem.internalization_factor += (1.0 - chem.internalization_factor) * intern_turnover;
4431            // Floor at 0.45: maximum internalization is ~55%, per
4432            // the literature on agonist-induced receptor endocytosis:
4433            // - β2AR (epinephrine, full agonist, 30 min): 55%
4434            //   internalized (January et al., JBC 1997)
4435            // - MOR (DAMGO, 30 min): 50-58% internalized
4436            //   (Science Advances, 2025)
4437            // - β2AR (weak agonists): 0-17% — internalization is
4438            //   agonist-dependent
4439            // - Cardiac β-AR (isoproterenol): ~50% lost in 10 min,
4440            //   85% recycled in 20 min (rapid recovery)
4441            //   (Lohse et al., Circ Res 1984)
4442            // Previous 0.1 floor allowed 90% internalization, which
4443            // is far beyond any reported physiological maximum.
4444            chem.internalization_factor =
4445                crate::state::sanitize::finite_clamp(chem.internalization_factor, 0.45, 1.0);
4446        }
4447
4448        // Baseline adaptation: the set-point itself drifts toward the
4449        // sustained level. This is emotional development — chronic
4450        // stress raises the cortisol baseline (the system "expects" more
4451        // stress), chronic well-being raises the serotonin baseline.
4452        //
4453        // A restoring force toward the genetic default prevents permanent
4454        // drift. Without this, chronic stress could permanently raise the
4455        // cortisol baseline with no recovery — the system would "forget"
4456        // its healthy set-point.
4457        //
4458        // The restoring force is equal to the adaptation rate (100%),
4459        // not 20% as in v2/v3. The previous 20% ratio was too weak: when
4460        // cortisol was high (level=1.0) and baseline had drifted to 0.6,
4461        // the deviation force (0.4 * rate) overwhelmed the restoring
4462        // force (0.08 * rate), so the baseline kept rising and stress
4463        // became self-reinforcing. With equal forces, the baseline
4464        // reaches an equilibrium that's the average of the current level
4465        // and the genetic default — it still adapts to sustained states,
4466        // but can't run away from the healthy set-point. Only very
4467        // prolonged, sustained states will shift the baseline
4468        // significantly, and recovery happens naturally when the
4469        // stressor ends.
4470        //
4471        // (Ramsay & Woods, 2014 — homeostatic regulation of set-points)
4472        for chem in &mut self.chemicals {
4473            // Adenosine has its own dedicated sleep-pressure mechanism
4474            // (metabolic accumulation / glymphatic clearance) that
4475            // directly modifies its baseline. The general baseline
4476            // adaptation would fight with that mechanism — pulling the
4477            // adenosine baseline back toward the genetic default while
4478            // the sleep pressure mechanism is trying to raise it. So
4479            // we skip adenosine here and let its dedicated mechanism
4480            // handle baseline drift.
4481            //
4482            // Cortisol and CRH are stress hormones with zero baseline
4483            // — their baselines should never adapt (they have no
4484            // homeostatic set-point, just a leaky integrator to zero).
4485            let chem_id = NeurochemicalId::from_u8(chem.id);
4486            if chem_id == NeurochemicalId::Adenosine
4487                || chem_id == NeurochemicalId::Cortisol
4488                || chem_id == NeurochemicalId::CRH
4489            {
4490                continue;
4491            }
4492            let deviation = chem.level - chem.baseline;
4493            let genetic_baseline = chem_id.default_baseline();
4494            let base_rate = baseline_adaptation_rate * dt_scale;
4495            // Restoring force toward the genetic baseline. The rate is
4496            // now 10x slower than the original, preventing the
4497            // self-reinforcing trap where high levels push baselines up,
4498            // weakening the homeostatic restoring force. The slower
4499            // rate gives the homeostatic force time to pull levels
4500            // toward genetic baselines before the baselines can adapt.
4501            let restoring = (genetic_baseline - chem.baseline) * base_rate;
4502            chem.baseline += deviation * base_rate + restoring;
4503            chem.baseline = crate::state::sanitize::finite_clamp(chem.baseline, 0.05, 0.95);
4504        }
4505
4506        // ─── Metaplasticity: the coupling matrix self-modifies ───
4507        //
4508        // The coupling matrix defines how neurochemicals influence
4509        // each other. Metaplasticity means this matrix itself is
4510        // plastic — it adapts based on sustained emotional states.
4511        //
4512        // This is "the system learning how to learn." Chronic stress
4513        // reshapes how neurochemicals interact, making the system
4514        // less reactive (allostatic adaptation). Sustained wellbeing
4515        // makes it more responsive.
4516        //
4517        // Biological grounding:
4518        // - Chronic stress alters receptor expression and signaling
4519        //   pathways, changing how neurotransmitters interact (McEwen,
4520        //   2007). The amygdala becomes hyperreactive, the PFC
4521        //   becomes hyporeactive — the coupling structure changes.
4522        // - Sustained positive states strengthen synaptic connections
4523        //   (long-term potentiation is modulated by dopamine and
4524        //   serotonin).
4525        //
4526        // The adaptation uses TONIC levels (sustained over minutes),
4527        // not momentary levels, so it responds to emotional history
4528        // rather than transient fluctuations.
4529        //
4530        // The process is gated by plasticity_gate — metaplasticity
4531        // only occurs when BDNF is high and cortisol is low (the
4532        // system is in a learning-capable state). Chronic stress
4533        // suppresses BDNF, which suppresses plasticity_gate, which
4534        // suppresses metaplasticity — a protective mechanism that
4535        // prevents stress from permanently rewiring the brain.
4536        // Recovery (BDNF restoration via serotonin) re-enables
4537        // metaplasticity, allowing the system to re-adapt.
4538        //
4539        // A restoring force toward the default matrix prevents
4540        // permanent drift, modeling homeostatic regulation of the
4541        // coupling structure (Ramsay & Woods, 2014).
4542        let meta_rate = params.metaplasticity_rate;
4543        if meta_rate > 0.0 && self.plasticity_gate > 0.1 {
4544            // Emotional context from tonic (sustained) levels,
4545            // measured as deviation from baseline. This ensures
4546            // neutral states produce zero adaptation signal.
4547            let cort_idx = NeurochemicalId::Cortisol as usize;
4548            let da_idx_m = NeurochemicalId::Dopamine as usize;
4549            let srt_idx_m = NeurochemicalId::Serotonin as usize;
4550
4551            let cort_tonic = self.chemicals[cort_idx].tonic_level;
4552            let cort_base = self.chemicals[cort_idx].baseline;
4553            let da_tonic = self.chemicals[da_idx_m].tonic_level;
4554            let da_base = self.chemicals[da_idx_m].baseline;
4555            let srt_tonic = self.chemicals[srt_idx_m].tonic_level;
4556            let srt_base = self.chemicals[srt_idx_m].baseline;
4557
4558            // Stress signal: cortisol tonic above baseline → stress
4559            // Wellbeing signal: DA + SRT tonic above baseline, cortisol at/below baseline
4560            //
4561            // A small dead zone prevents sub-threshold tonic drift from
4562            // producing spurious metaplasticity. Metaplasticity should
4563            // respond to sustained, meaningful emotional deviations, not
4564            // numerical noise or slow equilibrium drift.
4565            let dead_zone = 0.05;
4566            let stress =
4567                crate::state::sanitize::finite_clamp(cort_tonic - cort_base - dead_zone, 0.0, 1.0);
4568            let wellbeing =
4569                (crate::state::sanitize::finite_clamp(da_tonic - da_base - dead_zone, 0.0, 1.0)
4570                    + crate::state::sanitize::finite_clamp(
4571                        srt_tonic - srt_base - dead_zone,
4572                        0.0,
4573                        1.0,
4574                    ))
4575                    * 0.5
4576                    * (1.0 - stress);
4577
4578            // Gate by plasticity (BDNF-dependent)
4579            let gate = self.plasticity_gate * meta_rate * dt_scale;
4580
4581            for (i, (default_row, current_row)) in DEFAULT_COUPLING_MATRIX
4582                .iter()
4583                .zip(self.coupling_matrix.iter_mut())
4584                .enumerate()
4585            {
4586                for (j, (&default_val, current)) in
4587                    default_row.iter().zip(current_row.iter_mut()).enumerate()
4588                {
4589                    if i == j {
4590                        continue;
4591                    }
4592                    if default_val == 0.0 {
4593                        continue;
4594                    }
4595
4596                    // Restoring force toward default (75% of adaptation).
4597                    // Strengthened relative to the emotional stress/wellbeing
4598                    // adjustments so that sub-threshold drift and feedback
4599                    // loops cannot permanently lock the matrix away from
4600                    // its homeostatic set-point.
4601                    let restoring = (default_val - *current) * gate * 0.75;
4602
4603                    // Stress adaptation: weaken excitatory (positive)
4604                    // couplings, strengthen inhibitory (negative) ones.
4605                    // The brain becomes less reactive under chronic
4606                    // stress — excitatory pathways are dampened, while
4607                    // inhibitory pathways are reinforced to maintain
4608                    // stability.
4609                    //
4610                    // Both adjustments are negative: excitatory couplings
4611                    // are reduced (toward zero), and inhibitory couplings
4612                    // are made more negative (away from zero). The
4613                    // inhibitory strengthening is half the excitatory
4614                    // weakening, matching the asymmetry in the original
4615                    // design.
4616                    let stress_adj = if *current > 0.0 {
4617                        -stress * gate * current.abs()
4618                    } else {
4619                        -stress * gate * current.abs() * 0.5
4620                    };
4621
4622                    // Wellbeing adaptation: strengthen excitatory
4623                    // couplings slightly. The brain becomes more
4624                    // responsive when things are going well.
4625                    let wellbeing_adj = if *current > 0.0 {
4626                        wellbeing * gate * 0.5
4627                    } else {
4628                        0.0
4629                    };
4630
4631                    *current += restoring + stress_adj + wellbeing_adj;
4632
4633                    // Clamp to prevent extreme values
4634                    let max = default_val.abs().max(0.5) * 2.0;
4635                    *current = crate::state::sanitize::finite_clamp(*current, -max, max);
4636                }
4637            }
4638        }
4639
4640        // Recompute all derived fields with the new state
4641        self.recompute_derived_with_dt(dt_scale, params.network_levels);
4642    }

4916    /// (desensitization + internalization), and sleep reverses it.
4917    ///
4918    /// The daemon sets this to a nonzero value during sleep (NREM/REM
4919    /// or Sleeping zone) and 0.0 during wakefulness. The rate is
4920    /// calibrated so that a full sleep cycle (~90 minutes biological
4921    /// time) can restore receptors from maximum burnout (0.2
4922    /// desensitization, 0.45 internalization) back to near 1.0.
4923    ///
4924    /// Without this, the system enters a "receptor burnout trap":
4925    /// chronic stress elevates raw levels → receptors downregulate →
4926    /// effective levels collapse → but raw levels stay high (above
4927    /// the drifted baselines) → desensitization can't recover because
4928    /// the deviation is still positive. Sleep breaks this trap by
4929    /// restoring receptors unconditionally.

4932    /// sensitivity; Raczkowski et al., 2018 — sleep restores
4933    /// adrenergic receptor sensitivity)

4939    /// sleep-wake network owns. 0.0 = darkness or no eye: nothing
4940    /// suppressed.
4941    pub light_lux: f32,

4943    /// acetylcholine reports (Yu & Dayan, Neuron 2005): the body's

4945    /// fills it from the inference engine's tracked precision via
